To make the collapse explicit, normalize the town's fiscal
reachability volume to $V_F(1880) = 100$ and let
$\tau = t - 1880$ denote years since founding. Two forces erode
$V_F$ steadily across the 1880s: the slow foreclosure of
alternatives described above (an exponential term, since each
year of specialization compounds the last) and the linear
accumulation of fixed claims --- rent, company-store debt, and
deferred maintenance obligations --- against a wage base that is
not growing to match them:
\[
  V_F(\tau) = 100\,e^{-0.04\tau} - 1.5\tau,
  \qquad 0 \le \tau \le 13.
\]
This gives a smooth, unremarkable-looking decline across the
town's first thirteen years:
\[
  \begin{array}{c|cccc}
    \text{Year} & 1880 & 1885 & 1890 & 1893 \\
    \tau & 0 & 5 & 10 & 13 \\
    V_F(\tau) & 100.0 & 74.4 & 52.0 & 40.0
  \end{array}
\]
Nothing in this trajectory up to 1893 looks like an emergency.
$V_F$ is declining, but slowly, and the company's own
projection $\hat{P}$ --- tracking revenue and the contractual
return on housing --- shows no comparable deterioration, since
none of $\hat{P}$'s tracked quantities are the ones actually
eroding.

The 1893 wage cut is a discrete shock layered onto this baseline,
and its effect is disproportionate to its nominal size precisely
because rent is a fixed claim against a shrinking wage. Consider
a representative worker earning $10$ units before the cut, paying
a fixed rent of $2$ units, leaving $8$ units of discretionary
reach. A twenty-five percent wage cut reduces the wage to $7.5$;
with rent unchanged, discretionary reach falls to $5.5$ --- a
contraction of $31.25\
the shock. Applying this amplification factor to the town-level
trajectory:
\[
  V_F(1894) = V_F(13) \times \frac{5.5}{8} \approx 40.0 \times 0.6875
  \approx 27.5.
\]
By the Boundary Exhaustion Theorem (\cref{thm:boundary-exhaust}),
this matters more than the raw number suggests. Fiscal boundary
proximity $\beta_F(p,t)$ (\cref{defn:fiscal-boundary}) was
already small after thirteen years of steady erosion; the 1894
shock pushes the town toward $\beta_F \to 0$, the regime in which
small further disturbances produce disproportionately large
reductions in $V_F$ and the system's sensitivity to initial
conditions diverges.


Collapse


The disproportionate-sensitivity prediction is not abstract here.
The proximate trigger for the 1894 strike was not a policy
announcement but the dismissal of three members of a grievance
committee on May 10 --- a single administrative action,
small by any ordinary measure. By May 11 the shops were empty.
By July, federal troops occupied the town, the strike had been
broken, and the American Railway Union that had carried it
beyond Pullman's gates nationally was effectively destroyed
\parencite{lindsey1942}. Exactly as the Boundary Exhaustion
Theorem predicts, a disturbance with no special magnitude of its
own produced an outcome --- a national rail strike, federal
military intervention, dozens of deaths --- entirely
disproportionate to its size, because it landed on a system
already at $\beta_F \approx 0$.

In the narrowest sense the company won: workers returned on the
company's terms, wages were not restored. But the Governance
Capacity Theorem (\cref{thm:gov-capacity}) is stated over
$\liminf_{t\to\infty}$, not over any single negotiation, and on
that horizon the town did not survive. The State of Illinois sued
in August 1894, arguing the company had no charter right to
operate a town; the Illinois Supreme Court agreed in October
1898 and ordered the company to divest every non-industrial
holding, a process not completed until 1907
\parencite{buder1967}. George Pullman died in 1897, a year before
the ruling that dissolved his model town as a going concern.


What \texorpdfstring{$G_F + R_F \ge L_F$
{GF + RF >= LF} Would Have Required}

The Governance Capacity Theorem states that long-run viability
requires option generation plus institutional repair to keep pace
with constraint accumulation. Pullman's town violated this on
both terms simultaneously, not one. $G_F$ was suppressed by
design: no competing employer, landlord, or retailer was ever
permitted to enter the reachable set. $R_F$ was suppressed by
capture: a town government existed in name, its offices filled
by company loyalists, contributing nothing to genuine institutional
repair when the crisis came. Against this, $L_F$ --- rent fixed
against a return target, wages cut without renegotiation, debt
accumulating at the company store --- proceeded unopposed. No
version of this chapter's formalism requires a company town to
fail; it requires only that $G_F + R_F \ge L_F$ eventually, which
is a claim about the existence of working repair and option-
generating mechanisms, not about the prosperity of the firm
funding them. Pullman's town had neither, by the same design
choices that made it, for thirteen quiet years, look like a
model.


A Caution About the Reconstruction


This worked example uses real dates, real figures, and a real
documented outcome, but the closed-form trajectory
$V_F(\tau) = 100e^{-0.04\tau} - 1.5\tau$ is an illustrative
fit chosen to produce a clean, legible curve, not a quantity
recovered from archival data. No contemporary source reports a
``fiscal reachability volume'' for Pullman, Illinois, because the
concept did not exist in 1894. What the historical record does
support, without qualification, is the qualitative shape the toy
trajectory is built to display: steady, low-visibility erosion
of optionality; a fixed claim that amplifies a shock rather than
absorbing it; an administrative projection blind to the exact
quantity collapsing; and a small disturbance landing on an
exhausted boundary to produce an outcome wildly disproportionate
to its own size. The arithmetic is illustrative. The pattern is
not.


Empirical Estimation and Proxy Variables

\label{sec:ch27-proxies}

The formal variables $G_F$, $R_F$, $L_F$, and $V_F$ are
well-defined within the framework. A practitioner will ask
how to estimate them for a real jurisdiction. No proxy is
exact, but the framework is empirically approachable: each
variable maps to observable fiscal quantities that track
the same structure the formal variable represents.

\paragraph{Capacity field $\Phi$ and productive capacity.}
$\Phi(x,t)$ measures stored distinction-producing capacity
at position $x$ and time $t$. In fiscal terms, the closest
observable is \emph{broad economic capacity}: GDP per capita,
tax-base breadth, diversification of revenue sources, and
capital-stock quality. Jurisdictions with concentrated
revenue bases (a single dominant industry, a single large
employer) have low $\Phi$ relative to their nominal income.
Proxy: revenue concentration index, sectoral diversity of
the tax base.

\paragraph{Constraint field $S$ and administrative complexity.}
$S(x,t)$ measures accumulated constraint on future fiscal
options. The closest observable is \emph{mandatory
expenditure burden}: fixed debt-service obligations, unfunded
pension liabilities, constitutionally or legally mandated
spending floors. These function exactly like $S$: they
accumulate over time, are not easily reversed, and reduce
the set of feasible future budget allocations without
appearing on any single-year balance sheet as an emergency.
Proxy: fixed-obligation ratio (mandatory spending as share
of revenue), debt-service coverage ratio.

\paragraph{Reachability volume $V_F$ and scenario count.}
$V_F(p,t,\tau)$ is the admissible policy space accessible
within horizon $\tau$. The closest operational analogue is
\emph{fiscal scenario breadth}: the number of budget
allocations satisfying all binding constraints and
maintaining a primary balance path consistent with debt
sustainability. This can be estimated by scenario
simulation: for a given jurisdiction and horizon, enumerate
feasible combinations of tax rates, spending levels, and
borrowing within legal and market constraints. The volume
of this set --- the number of feasible scenarios --- is
a proxy for $V_F$.

\paragraph{Flow variables $G_F$, $R_F$, $L_F$.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $G_F$ (option generation): growth in tax-base
        breadth, new revenue instruments available,
        increase in institutional flexibility (e.g.\
        new borrowing facilities, expanded tax authority).
  \item $R_F$ (repair capacity): debt-restructuring
        access, reserve fund levels, credit-market
        access, and the presence of independent fiscal
        institutions capable of revising policy without
        political capture.
  \item $L_F$ (option loss): growth in fixed-obligation
        liabilities, reduction in credit ratings,
        constitutional spending mandates added,
        narrowing of politically feasible budget space.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}{Proxy limitations}{proxy-limits}
  These proxies capture the formal variables
  approximately. A jurisdiction with high GDP but a
  single-commodity revenue base has high nominal $\Phi$
  but low structural $\Phi$; a jurisdiction with low
  nominal debt but high pension liabilities has low
  $S$ on the balance sheet but high effective $S$ in
  the framework's terms. The right proxies depend on
  data availability and the specific failure modes being
  diagnosed. The framework provides the conceptual
  structure; the mapping to observables requires
  domain knowledge and judgment.
\end{remark}

  \item Administrative projections produce blind spots

\end{chapsummary}

Governance and Future Possibility


The standard economic account of government concerns
the correction of market failures: public goods,
externalities, information asymmetries. This account
is important but structurally incomplete. It treats
governance as a set of interventions into an otherwise
functioning system of private choices. It does not
address the prior question of what makes that system
of private choices coherent, stable, and reversible.

Institutional economics comes closer. Treating institutions
as the rules of the game that structure human interaction
already moves past pure market-failure correction toward
something resembling this chapter's concerns
\parencite{north1990}. What it does not provide is a
geometry: a way of measuring, rather than only qualitatively
describing, how much a given set of rules expands or
contracts the space of futures still reachable from where a
polity currently stands.

The distinction-theoretic account begins elsewhere.
Governance is the set of institutions, norms, and
processes that preserve the collective ability to make
and revise choices. Its primary function is not to
make choices but to maintain the space within which
choices remain meaningful. A government that achieves
a particular policy outcome by eliminating the
possibility of future policy revision has not governed
well — it has extracted from the future.

This reframing makes governance a special case of
the Generative Admissibility Principle
(\cref{prc:gap-ch15}): governance is valuable insofar
as it preserves the admissible volume of collective
future possibility.


Governance as Preservation of Reachable Futures

\label{sec:ch28-preservation}

Governance is frequently understood, implicitly, as
optimisation toward a predetermined objective: a five-year
plan, a target growth rate, a fixed policy programme to be
implemented as faithfully as possible. Under conditions of
deep uncertainty this understanding is not merely incomplete
but unworkable, because the objective itself may later
require revision in light of information that does not yet
exist. A government that optimises hard toward a fixed target
chosen today has, by the same act, committed itself to
ignoring whatever the future turns out to actually require.

A government therefore faces a higher-order task than
optimisation toward any single objective:
\[
  \mathcal{G}^* = \arg\max_{\mathcal{G}} \Vol(R_t),
\]
where $R_t$ is the future reachable policy space and the
maximisation is over governance structures $\mathcal{G}$
rather than over policies within a fixed structure. The
quality of governance, on this reading, is not measured
solely by current policy outcomes but by the preservation of
the capacity to choose well once more is known. This is why
institutional capacity, information-gathering infrastructure,
distributed expertise, and flexible rather than rigidly
pre-committed authority recur as priorities across
well-functioning governance systems: each is an investment in
$\Vol(R_t)$ rather than in any particular predicted outcome,
exactly as Chapter~\ref{ch:fiscal}'s treatment of $G_F$ and
$R_F$ already formalises for the fiscal case specifically.


What Would Falsify This Realization

\label{sec:ch28-falsifiability}

The Institutional Repair Theorem, proved later in this
chapter (\cref{thm:inst-repair}), characterises generative
admissibility for an institution by three conditions ---
coverage, meta-repair, and admissibility of individual
repairs --- and its proof derives each condition directly
from general machinery: the Regeneration Theorem, the
Regenerative Expansion Theorem, and the repair-admissibility
condition of Chapter~\ref{ch:repair}. This is falsifiable
against a specific test: if governing institutions required a
repair criterion not already implied by this general
machinery --- some condition special to collective political
life that the abstract theory of repair could not have
anticipated --- the claim that governance realizes
Chapter~\ref{ch:repair}'s repair theory, rather than applying
its vocabulary to a domain it does not actually fit, would
fail. The proof given later introduces no such condition.


What This Chapter Establishes

\label{sec:ch28-preview}

By the end of this chapter, governance will have been defined
as a distinction ecology over collective policy space, the
Governance Threshold and Option Preservation Theorems will
characterise when governance structures are generatively
admissible, and the Institutional Repair Theorem will identify
institutional health with the same repair conditions developed
in general in Chapter~\ref{ch:repair}. Chapter~\ref{ch:science-regen}
then turns from governance in general to one institution in
particular: the scientific enterprise, whose capacity to
revise its own foundational commitments is among the clearest
real-world instances of the meta-repair condition this chapter
identifies as the hardest to satisfy.

\begin{definition}{Governance Ecology}{gov-ecology}
  \index{admissibility!social}
  A \emph{governance ecology} is a distinction ecology
  $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{D}_G, \mathcal{L}_G,
  \mathcal{R}_G)$ where:
  \begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathcal{D}_G$ is the set of institutional
          distinctions: the categories, norms, roles,
          and procedures that structure collective action.
    \item $\mathcal{L}_G$ is the dependency relation
          among institutional distinctions.
    \item $\mathcal{R}_G$ is the repair algebra:
          the set of reform, revision, and correction
          mechanisms available to the polity.
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{distinctionaside}{The Form That Creates the Problem It
  Measures}
A government agency introduces a reporting requirement to
monitor compliance.

The reporting requirement generates administrative work. The
administrative work produces errors and delays. The errors
and delays look, on a dashboard, like a compliance problem.

The compliance problem justifies a new reporting requirement.

Nobody designed this loop. Nobody benefits from it
especially. The measurement apparatus is reproducing itself,
one justified requirement at a time --- another instance of
the rule-doubling dynamic from Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction-ecology}'s
bureaucratic growth, now run by the institution on itself.
\end{distinctionaside}


The Governance Threshold Theorem


\begin{theorem}{Governance Threshold Theorem}{gov-threshold}
  A governance ecology $\mathcal{G}$ has positive
  long-term collective reachability iff
  the rate of institutional distinction generation
  and repair exceeds the rate of institutional erosion:
  \[
    \liminf_{t\to\infty}
    V_G(p_G, t) > 0
    \;\iff\;
    G_G(t) + R_G(t) \ge L_G(t)
    \quad \text{eventually},
  \]
  where $V_G$ is the collective reachability volume in
  policy space, $G_G$ is institutional innovation,
  $R_G$ is institutional repair, and $L_G$ is
  institutional erosion.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The governance ecology is a special case of the
  distinction ecology of Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction-ecology}.
  By the Regenerative Ecology Theorem
  (\cref{thm:regen-ecology-thm}), positive long-term
  viability requires $G_G + R_G \ge L_G$ eventually.
  The Universal Regeneration Theorem
  (\cref{thm:universal-regen}) then gives the
  equivalence with $\liminf V_G > 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{What institutional erosion looks like}
  {inst-erosion}
  Institutional erosion $L_G$ includes: regulatory
  capture (distinctions between public interest and
  private interest collapse); norm erosion (distinctions
  between acceptable and unacceptable conduct become
  ambiguous); administrative degradation (distinctions
  between competent and incompetent public service
  disappear); and constitutional hollowing
  (formal institutional distinctions remain but
  the repair mechanisms that enforce them atrophy).
  In each case the mechanism is the same:
  a distinction that once structured collective action
  loses its recoverability.
\end{remark}


The Option Preservation Theorem


\begin{theorem}{Option Preservation Theorem}{option-preserve}
  A governance action $a: P \to P$ is \emph{option-preserving}
  iff
  \[
    V_G(a(p), t) \;\ge\; V_G(p, t).
  \]
  Among all governance actions achieving the same
  immediate policy outcome $a(p) = q$, the
  option-preserving action dominates all extractive
  alternatives over sufficiently long horizons.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Future Preservation Theorem
  (\cref{thm:future-preserve}), among all trajectories
  achieving equal immediate reward, those with
  non-decreasing $V_G$ dominate extractive alternatives
  for any reward function non-decreasing in $V_G$.
  Policy outcomes that citizens value are, in any
  plausible social welfare function, non-decreasing
  in collective reachability volume (since reachability
  volume bounds the set of achievable future outcomes).
  Therefore option-preserving governance actions
  dominate extractive ones over long horizons.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}{Constitutional Entrenchment vs.\ Flexibility}
  {constitutional}
  Constitutional design involves a tradeoff between
  entrenchment and flexibility.
  Fully entrenched constitutions constrain future
  majorities but also constrain future repair.
  Fully flexible constitutions permit repair but also
  permit erasure of options.

  The Option Preservation Theorem provides a formal
  criterion: a constitutional provision is justified
  by option preservation iff it protects against
  extractive actions that would otherwise reduce $V_G$,
  and does not itself reduce $V_G$ below the level
  achievable without the provision.
  Rights that protect minority distinctions (linguistic,
  religious, cultural) are typically option-preserving:
  they prevent majority erasure of distinction diversity.
  Rights that entrench current majority preferences
  (e.g.\ constitutional property protection for
  existing concentrations) are potentially option-reducing.
\end{example}


Premature Collapse and Regulatory Projection

\label{sec:ch28-projection}
\index{collapse!institutional collapse|textbf}

A specific and recurring failure mode of option-reducing
governance deserves its own treatment: regulation written in
anticipation of an uncertain future technology or practice,
before that future has actually arrived. The regulator does
not observe the future technological state space $X$
directly. It observes a projection of it, $\pi(X)$ ---
a simplified, present-day model of what the technology will
become, built from current trends, current incentives, and
whatever experts are willing to commit to in testimony. This
is projection in exactly the sense of
Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction}: a partition of a richer space
collapsed onto a coarser one, with the Blind Spot Theorem's
guarantee that some genuine distinctions in $X$ are not
visible in $\pi(X)$ at all.

The danger specific to regulation is that policy is then
optimised against $\pi(X)$ rather than against $X$ itself,
and --- unlike most projections, which are merely descriptive
--- regulatory projections are typically written into binding
rules. The resulting distortion can be measured directly in
this chapter's terms:
\[
  D_A(\pi)
  = d\bigl(\adm(X), \adm(\pi(X))\bigr),
\]
the distance between the admissibility manifold the actual
future technology would support and the admissibility
manifold the regulator's simplified projection supports.
Where $D_A(\pi)$ is large, future admissible trajectories ---
uses of the technology that would have remained open under
accurate foresight --- become inaccessible not because they
were judged and rejected, but because the regulatory
framework was built around a projection that never
distinguished them from something else.

This is the formal content behind a familiar complaint about
anticipatory regulation: that committing early to a rigid
framework for an uncertain technology tends to entrench the
assumptions of the moment the framework was written, at
exactly the moment those assumptions are least reliable. The
Option Preservation Theorem's criterion applies directly.
A regulatory provision justified by $\pi(X)$ alone, without
independent verification that $D_A(\pi)$ is small, has no
guarantee of being option-preserving with respect to the $X$
that actually arrives --- and the Administrative Blind Spot
Theorem of Chapter~\ref{ch:fiscal} shows this is not a
correctable oversight but a structural property of governing
by projection at all.


The Institutional Repair Theorem


\begin{theorem}{Institutional Repair Theorem}{inst-repair}
  An institution $\mathcal{I}$ is generatively
  admissible iff its internal repair mechanisms
  $\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{I}$ satisfy:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item \emph{Coverage}: every distinction in
          $\mathcal{D}_\mathcal{I}$ is within the
          repair capacity of $\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{I}$.
    \item \emph{Meta-repair}: $\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{I}$
          can repair itself — the institution has
          mechanisms for reforming its reform mechanisms.
    \item \emph{Admissibility}: every repair in
          $\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{I}$ preserves or
          increases $V_G$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Regeneration Theorem (\cref{thm:regeneration}),
  a system is regenerative iff it preserves repair
  capacity and repair is continuously exercisable.
  (i) Coverage ensures no distinction falls outside
  the repair capacity.
  (ii) Meta-repair is the Regenerative Expansion Theorem
  (\cref{thm:regen-expansion}) applied to $\mathcal{R}$:
  it ensures the repair algebra itself can grow when
  novel anomalies arise.
  (iii) Admissibility of individual repairs is the
  condition of \cref{prop:repair-adm}: repair does not
  reduce reachability volume.
  Together these three conditions are necessary and
  sufficient for generative admissibility of
  $\mathcal{I}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Why (ii) is the hardest condition}{meta-repair}
  The meta-repair condition (ii) is routinely violated
  by institutions that encounter persistent anomalies
  in their own functioning but cannot revise their
  reform mechanisms. A legislature that cannot reform
  its own procedures, a court that cannot update its
  interpretive frameworks, a bureaucracy that cannot
  restructure its own incentives — all satisfy (i)
  and (iii) for existing distinctions but fail (ii)
  when those distinctions generate persistent anomalies.
  The Scientific Revolution Theorem
  (\cref{thm:sci-rev-thm}) applies directly:
  institutional saturation plus deep persistent
  anomalies requires ontological enlargement of the
  institutional distinction manifold.
\end{remark}


Institutional Regeneration

\label{sec:ch28-inst-regen}

The meta-repair condition is worth isolating as a quantity in
its own right, since it is precisely what distinguishes an
institution that merely survives from one that regenerates.
Define the \emph{institutional regenerative capacity}
\[
  \Gamma_\mathcal{I}
  = \frac{d}{dt} \Vol(R_\mathcal{I}),
\]
where $R_\mathcal{I}$ is the institution's future reachable
policy space. An institution with $\Gamma_\mathcal{I} \ge 0$
is regenerative in the sense of Chapter~\ref{ch:regeneration};
an institution with $\Gamma_\mathcal{I} < 0$ is consuming its
own future optionality, regardless of how well it is
currently performing against any present-day metric.

This sharpens a distinction easy to blur in practice: between
an institution that survives by avoiding disruptive change ---
mere incrementalism, holding $R_\mathcal{I}$ roughly constant
by attempting nothing --- and one that actively invests in
expanding $R_\mathcal{I}$. The Regenerative Expansion Theorem
(\cref{thm:regen-expansion}) already establishes that
$\kappa_\Sigma$ does not grow by default; it grows only under
specific conditions on available resources and repair
diversity. The institutional case is the same. An institution
that merely avoids breaking things is not thereby regenerating
its meta-repair capacity, and an institution that survives
this way for a long time can still be quietly converging on
$\Gamma_\mathcal{I} = 0$ or below, with the shortfall invisible
until a persistent anomaly arrives that its frozen reform
mechanisms cannot accommodate. Regeneration, here as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:regeneration}, is something an institution
must actively do, not a default state preserved by inaction.


The Democratic Reachability Theorem


\begin{theorem}{Democratic Reachability Theorem}{dem-reach}
  Among governance systems with equal decision-making
  efficiency, democracy maximises long-term collective
  reachability volume $V_G$ iff it satisfies:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item \emph{Distinction diversity}: it maintains
          multiple independent institutional distinction
          systems (parties, courts, press, civil society).
    \item \emph{Repair accessibility}: the repair
          algebra $\mathcal{R}_G$ is accessible to
          all members of the polity, not only incumbents.
    \item \emph{Meta-repair preservation}: the
          mechanisms for revising governance structures
          are themselves protected from extraction
          by current majorities.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Diversity--Repair Theorem
  (\cref{thm:diversity-repair}), (i) maximises repair
  capacity by providing independent pathways for
  distinction restoration.

  By the Repair Existence Theorem
  (\cref{thm:repair-exist}), repair is possible only
  when recoverability is positive. (ii) ensures that
  recoverability is distributed: if repair is
  accessible only to incumbents, the recoverability of
  distinctions disadvantageous to incumbents may be
  driven to zero.

  By the Institutional Repair Theorem, (iii) is
  necessary for generative admissibility.

  Together (i)--(iii) maximise $V_G$ over time:
  (i) generates the widest repair capacity; (ii) ensures
  that capacity is exercised across the full distinction
  space; (iii) prevents the repair algebra from being
  captured and narrowed by incumbent actors.
  No governance system with fewer of these properties
  can maintain equivalent $V_G$ under the same
  shock distribution.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{The theorem's conditional scope}{dem-reach-scope}
  The Democratic Reachability Theorem is a conditional
  result. It states what conditions a governance system
  must satisfy to maximise collective reachability volume
  under the stated assumptions --- not that democracy as
  any particular historical institution necessarily
  satisfies these conditions, nor that reachability
  maximisation is the uniquely correct criterion for
  governance. The three conditions (distinction diversity,
  repair accessibility, meta-repair preservation) are
  structural requirements. Whether a given polity calling
  itself democratic actually satisfies them is an
  empirical question, and the theorem does not prejudge
  it. Governance systems that are formally democratic
  but have substantially violated (ii) or (iii) may
  have lower $V_G$ than systems with different formal
  names that satisfy all three conditions in practice.
  The theorem provides criteria, not a verdict.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}{Democratic backsliding as reachability collapse}
  {backsliding}
  Democratic backsliding is the process by which
  condition (iii) is progressively violated: incumbents
  use their current majority to modify the meta-repair
  mechanisms (electoral rules, judicial independence,
  press freedom, civil society regulation) in ways that
  reduce the repair accessibility of future minorities.

  The Boundary Exhaustion Theorem
  (\cref{thm:boundary-exhaust}) applies: as backsliding
  proceeds, $\beta_G \to 0$. Small additional steps
  produce disproportionate reductions in $V_G$. The
  admissible repair set contracts toward the empty set.
  At the limit, the polity has passed its governance
  semantic horizon: the repair distinctions that would
  permit reversal have crossed into irrecoverability.
\end{remark}


Related Frameworks

\label{sec:ch28-related-frameworks}

\subsection*{Von Foerster: Second-Order Cybernetics}

\emph{Convergence.} Chapter~\ref{ch:repair} engaged cybernetics
through Wiener's feedback regulation and
Chapter~\ref{ch:regeneration} through Ashby's ultrastability;
this chapter's concern is closer to von Foerster's second-order
move. Von Foerster insists that any observer or regulator is
itself embedded in, and inseparable from, the system it observes
and regulates --- a cybernetics of observing systems, not a
cybernetics of systems observed from a neutral outside
\parencite{foerster1960}. This is precisely the structure of this
chapter's regulatory projection problem: a regulator does not
stand outside the technological state space $X$ producing a
neutral description of it; its projection $\pi(X)$ is itself
drawn from within the system being governed, and that projection
then becomes binding on the system's own future evolution.

\emph{Divergence.} Von Foerster's framework is principally
diagnostic: it identifies observer-embeddedness as a ubiquitous
feature of regulatory and cognitive systems without offering a
way to measure how costly a given act of self-involved
observation turns out to be. The regulatory distortion measure
introduced above, $D_A(\pi) = d(\adm(X), \adm(\pi(X)))$
(\cref{sec:ch28-projection}), supplies exactly this missing
quantity: a concrete, computable distance between the
admissibility manifold the real system supports and the one the
regulator's self-involved projection supports, turning von
Foerster's qualitative warning into an operational diagnostic.

\emph{Open question.} Whether $D_A(\pi)$ is bounded below by some
general property of self-referential observation in von
Foerster's sense --- whether second-order embeddedness implies a
nonzero minimum projection distortion for any regulator that is
itself part of the regulated system --- or whether $D_A(\pi)$ can
in principle be driven to zero by sufficiently careful
institutional design, is not settled here.

\subsection*{Luhmann: Law and Politics as Closed Subsystems}

\emph{Convergence.} Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction-ecology} engaged
Luhmann on operational closure in general; this chapter's concern
is his more specific treatment of law and politics as
functionally differentiated, operationally closed subsystems of
society, each reproducing itself through its own code --- the
legal system through legal/illegal, the political system through
government/opposition --- with neighbouring subsystems able only
to perturb, not directly determine, each other's internal
operations \parencite{luhmann1990}. This matches the Governance
Ecology defined above (\cref{defn:gov-ecology}), which likewise
treats governance as having its own internal dynamics rather than
serving as a passive transmission mechanism for economic or
technical inputs.

\emph{Divergence.} For Luhmann, operational closure is close to
absolute: the legal system cannot be directly steered by
political will or economic incentive, only perturbed, because law
processes only legal communications. The Institutional Repair
Theorem and the Democratic Reachability Theorem
(\cref{thm:inst-repair,thm:dem-reach}) instead treat governance as
continuous with the rest of the distinction-ecology framework: an
institution's regenerative capacity $\Gamma_\mathcal{I}$
(\cref{sec:ch28-inst-regen}) is measured in exactly the same units
and by exactly the same apparatus as the regenerative capacity of
any other distinction ecology in this book, with no special
operational-closure boundary erected around the legal or
political subsystem.

\emph{Open question.} Whether Luhmann's strong closure claim is
compatible with, or actually contradicted by, the Democratic
Reachability Theorem's treatment of governance ecologies as
porous to the reachable-policy spaces of the economic and
technological systems they regulate, is not resolved here.


\section{Extended Example: The Newspaper That Optimized Itself to
Death}
\label{sec:ch28-newspaper}

The pattern this chapter formalizes --- institutional erosion
proceeding unopposed by repair, meta-repair specifically
disabled, and an institution that continues to exist in every
formal sense while its capacity to do the thing it exists for
quietly disappears --- has a documented, dated, and unusually
self-aware instance: the American newspaper industry's collapse
between roughly 2006 and 2018, and within it, the particular and
closely chronicled case of \emph{The Denver Post} under the
ownership of Alden Global Capital.


The Backdrop: An Industry-Wide Loss of \texorpdfstring{$G_G$
{GG}}

Some of what happened to American newspapers in this period is
not institutional failure in this chapter's sense at all --- it
is straightforward loss of revenue that no governance structure
could fully repair. Industry-wide advertising revenue, the
traditional source of $G_G$ for most newspapers, fell from
roughly forty-nine billion dollars in 2006 to fourteen billion
dollars in 2018, as classified and display advertising migrated
first to Craigslist and then to programmatic platforms
\parencite{pew2019newspapers}. This decline sets the stage but
does not by itself explain what follows. A revenue collapse of
this magnitude forces institutions to shrink; it does not by
itself determine \emph{how} they shrink, nor whether the
shrinking institution retains the capacity defined by the
Institutional Repair Theorem (\cref{thm:inst-repair}) to revise
its own response as conditions change. That question is
answered by ownership and governance structure, not by the
revenue line alone --- which is exactly why two newspapers
facing the same advertising collapse can end up in very
different places.


The Governance Ecology of a Newsroom


Cast as a governance ecology $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{D}_G,
\mathcal{L}_G, \mathcal{R}_G)$ (\cref{defn:gov-ecology}), a
newsroom's institutional distinctions $\mathcal{D}_G$ are its
beats: the city-hall reporter who knows which council aide
actually returns calls, the courts reporter with a standing
relationship to the clerk's office, the investigative team with
the legal review process needed to publish safely. The
dependency relation $\mathcal{L}_G$ is steep: a beat is not a
headcount, it is accumulated, largely non-transferable local
knowledge built over years, and a newly hired reporter does not
restore a beat's distinction capacity merely by filling its
title. The repair algebra $\mathcal{R}_G$ is hiring, training,
mentorship, and editorial-independence norms that let the
newsroom revise its own coverage priorities as the city changes.
None of this shows up as a separate line on a balance sheet; all
of it is what $V_G$ is actually tracking.


2010--2013: A Newsroom That Still Worked


In 2010, Alden Global Capital, a New York hedge fund, acquired a
controlling interest in MediaNews Group, the parent of Digital
First Media and, through it, \emph{The Denver Post}
\parencite{npr2018denverpost}. For the next several years the
newsroom kept functioning by the most visible test available: in
2013, a 142-person newsroom photographed together after the
paper won a Pulitzer Prize for its coverage of the 2012 Aurora
theater shooting --- the paper's ninth Pulitzer since its 1892
founding. By any present-day metric available in 2013, the
institution looked healthy.


Constitutional Hollowing


What happened next is close to a textbook instance of the
erosion this chapter calls \emph{constitutional hollowing}: formal
institutional distinctions remain in place while the repair
mechanisms that enforce them atrophy (\cref{rmk:inst-erosion}).
The newspaper's name did not change. Its masthead did not
change. It published every day. Its 1892 founding date and nine
Pulitzers remained, undiminished, part of its public identity.
What changed was the newsroom inside that identity: from more
than 250 staff in the years before Alden's acquisition, to fewer
than 100 by early 2018, to roughly 70 after a further round of
about 30 layoffs that April \parencite{npr2018denverpost}. Every
one of these reductions was an elimination of specific beats ---
specific accumulated, non-transferable distinctions in
$\mathcal{D}_G$ --- not a proportional thinning of an undifferentiated
mass. A beat eliminated this way does not return when conditions
improve; the reporter who held the relationships is gone, and
the relationships do not transfer to whoever is left.

\subsection{Reachability Collapse and Admissibility Collapse,
Distinguished}

It is worth separating two things this chapter treats as
related but distinct. The newspaper's \emph{reachability}
collapsed in the straightforward sense that specific futures ---
a long-form investigation into a state agency, sustained
coverage of a school district, an editor with enough institutional
memory to recognize that this year's story rhymes with one from
a decade ago --- became unreachable once the staff and
relationships that would have produced them were gone.
Separately, the newspaper's \emph{admissibility} collapsed in the
precise sense of the Option Preservation Theorem
(\cref{thm:option-preserve}): each round of layoffs was, in this
theorem's terms, an extractive action rather than an
option-preserving one, since each reduced $V_G(a(p), t)$ relative
to less aggressive alternatives available at the same
revenue target. The theorem does not claim extractive actions
are never taken; it claims they are dominated by option-preserving
alternatives over sufficiently long horizons, and identifies
exactly the quantity --- future reachable reporting capacity ---
that the extractive path was spending down.


Why the Institutional Repair Theorem Fails on All Three Conditions


The Institutional Repair Theorem (\cref{thm:inst-repair})
requires coverage, meta-repair, and admissibility together.
\emph{The Denver Post} under this ownership failed all three, not
just one. Coverage failed directly: as specific beats were
eliminated rather than redistributed, specific distinctions in
$\mathcal{D}_\mathcal{I}$ fell entirely outside the remaining
staff's repair capacity --- nobody was left to notice what was no
longer being covered, which is itself a blind spot in exactly the
sense of \cref{thm:admin-blind}, here operating on the
institution's own coverage rather than on government finances.
Admissibility failed by the argument above. Meta-repair, the
hardest condition (\cref{rmk:meta-repair}), failed most visibly: when
the newsroom's own staff attempted, through public criticism, to
revise the ownership's strategy in response to a clearly
persistent anomaly, the attempt was suppressed rather than
incorporated, as the next section describes. An institution that
cannot revise its own reform mechanism when that mechanism is
the thing producing the anomaly is, by this theorem, no longer
generatively admissible, regardless of whether it continues to
publish.


April 2018: An Attempt at Meta-Repair


On April 6, 2018, the day before another round of layoffs, the
Post's own editorial board published an editorial accusing its
owners of deliberately shrinking what the paper offered while
raising what it charged readers for the privilege, despite the
parent company remaining profitable, and calling on Alden to
sell the paper to an owner who would invest in it
\parencite{npr2018denverpost}. This was, in the chapter's terms, an
attempt at exactly the kind of internal, self-directed correction
the meta-repair condition requires: the institution recognizing
a persistent anomaly in its own functioning and trying to revise
the mechanism producing it. It failed. Within days, editorial
page editor Chuck Plunkett resigned after a follow-up piece was
blocked, telling colleagues he had been informed he was
``biting the hand that fed'' the newspaper. Three other senior
figures --- former owner Dean Singleton, and editors Dana
Coffield and Larry Ryckman --- resigned within the same stretch
of days. The repair mechanism had identified the failure
correctly and was overruled by the same ownership structure it
was trying to repair, which is precisely the failure mode
condition (ii) of the Institutional Repair Theorem is built to
detect.
