The most natural candidates are:
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
  \item \emph{Preference for existence:} any agent that
        exists and prefers to continue existing has
        instrumental reason to preserve admissible volume.
        This licenses the normative conclusion but only
        for existing agents with continuity preferences.
  \item \emph{Pareto improvement across time:} preserving
        admissible volume benefits all future agents
        without making any present agent worse off. This
        licenses a weak normative conclusion under
        contestable assumptions about future agent
        interests.
  \item \emph{Formal axiology:} a theory of value that
        takes option-preservation as intrinsically good.
        This would make the normative conclusion a theorem
        but requires the additional axiological premise to
        be independently motivated.
\end{enumerate}
None of these is supplied by this framework. The problem
is not a defect to be patched but a genuine frontier: the
point at which formal theory hands off to philosophy.


Operationalizing the Social Reachability Variables


Chapters~\ref{ch:fiscal}--\ref{ch:science-regen} use the
variables $G_F, R_F, L_F$ (fiscal generation, repair, loss),
$G_G, R_G, L_G$ (governance), and their scientific analogues
throughout formal theorems and worked examples. These variables
are well-defined within the abstract framework. What is not
yet established is a principled method for measuring them
in actual fiscal, governance, or scientific systems.

The difficulty is genuine. Consider the fiscal case.
$G_F$ is the rate at which new admissible policy options
are generated --- new revenue sources, new expenditure
instruments, new institutional arrangements that enlarge
the policy state space $P_T \times P_E \times P_I$
(\cref{defn:policy-state}). Candidate proxies include:
growth in the tax base, count of new revenue instruments
adopted, and legislative capacity utilization. But each
proxy captures only part of the theoretical quantity, and
the mapping from proxy to $G_F$ depends on assumptions
about what counts as an admissible option.

Similarly, $L_F$ (the rate of option loss) could be proxied
by: growth in fixed-obligation liabilities as a share of
revenue, reduction in the number of feasible budget
allocations satisfying all constraints, or credit-rating
deterioration. $R_F$ (repair capacity) might be proxied
by: debt-restructuring facility access, reserve fund levels,
or institutional flexibility in adjusting tax rates.

These are reasonable starting points, but no mapping from
proxy to formal variable has been established with
sufficient precision to validate the model against data.
Three research directions would close this gap:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item \emph{Agent-based calibration}: build a simulation
        in which $G_F, R_F, L_F$ are defined explicitly,
        and identify which aggregate statistics of the
        simulation correspond to observable fiscal data.
  \item \emph{Historical regression}: treat the trajectory
        $V_F(t)$ as a latent variable and use historical
        fiscal crisis data (municipal defaults,
        sovereign debt restructurings) to estimate the
        decay rate of $V_F$ in the years preceding
        collapse, then work backward to infer the
        implied values of $G_F, R_F, L_F$.
  \item \emph{Expert elicitation}: convene fiscal
        economists and public finance practitioners to
        estimate $G_F, R_F, L_F$ for specific cases,
        then check whether the framework's predictions
        (boundary proximity, amplification effects,
        collapse dynamics) match the observed outcomes.
\end{enumerate}
The same problem applies to governance ($V_G$) and science
($V_S$). Until this operationalization gap is closed, the
social reachability chapters make a claim about
\emph{structure} --- how fiscal, governance, and scientific
systems work in abstract --- rather than a claim about
\emph{specific systems}. The worked examples in
Chapters~27--29 are illustrative rather than validating.
