=========

A concept acquires meaning precisely insofar as it
partitions a domain: separating things that fall under
it from things that do not, cases that are clear from
cases that are ambiguous, extensions from intensions.
A word is semantically rich when the partition it
induces is fine, stable, and recoverable.
A word is semantically impoverished when its partition
is coarse, unstable, or has collapsed toward the
semantic horizon.

The dominant twentieth-century alternatives to a
structure-first account of meaning treat it either as a
relation between signs and abstract senses, fixed
independently of any geometric organisation
\parencite{frege1892}, or as constituted entirely by patterns
of use within a language game, with no commitment to any
common structure across uses at all \parencite{wittgenstein1953}.
Speech-act and implicature theories refine the second
tradition by locating meaning in what an utterance does
within a conversational exchange rather than what it denotes
\parencite{austin1962,grice1975}. None of these traditions is
wrong about the phenomena it describes, but none offers a
geometry: there is no answer, on a pure use-theory or
speech-act account, to the question of how far apart two
meanings are, whether a meaning's neighbourhood is densely or
sparsely populated by related meanings, or what it would take
to repair a meaning that has drifted.

This view has closer antecedents in conceptual space theory
\parencite{gardenfors2000conceptual}, which models
concepts as convex regions in quality dimensions.
The present framework extends this by:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item grounding conceptual spaces in the formal
        distinction machinery of Part~I;
  \item adding dynamics — meanings can be damaged and
        repaired;
  \item adding a geometric criterion for evaluating
        meanings — admissibility — that goes beyond
        mere accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmo-completion} closed the book's treatment
of physical realization by asking whether the admissibility
geometry of Part~V could organise meaning itself, not merely
matter, gravity, and cosmological history. This chapter is
the first attempt at an answer. If a meaning manifold is to
be more than a convenient visualisation, the same machinery
that governed reachable volume, curvature, and repair in the
physical case must reappear here, applied to a domain --- the
space of possible meanings --- where the units being measured
are not particles but distinctions in the sense of
Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction}.


What Would Falsify This Realization

\label{sec:ch20-falsifiability}

The claim that meaning is a genuine instance of admissibility
geometry, rather than a domain merely described using
admissibility's vocabulary, is falsifiable against a specific
structural test. The Meaning Repair Theorem
(\cref{thm:meaning-repair}), proved later in this chapter,
should be structurally identical to the Repair Existence and
Minimal Repair Theorems of Chapter~\ref{ch:repair}
(\cref{thm:repair-exist,thm:minimal-repair}) once restricted
to the semantic domain: the same existence condition
$\reco(m,t)>0$, the same minimality criteria, now applied to
meanings rather than arbitrary distinctions. If semantic
repair required additional machinery not already present in
the general theory of repair --- some mechanism specific to
meaning that the abstract theory could not have anticipated
--- the claim that semantic geometry realizes the book's
general framework, rather than merely echoing its language,
would fail.


Common Misinterpretations of Semantic Geometry

\label{sec:ch20-misinterpretations}

A first misreading takes this chapter to claim that meanings
are literally points in a physical or perceptual space ---
that the geometry is metaphorical packaging for what is
really just associative similarity. The semantic distance
function is a genuine metric in the technical sense, proved
to satisfy the relevant axioms in the Meaning Distance
Theorem, and the curvature, repair, and reachability results
built on it are theorems about that metric structure, not
loose analogies borrowed from physics for rhetorical effect.

A second misreading assumes that because meaning has a
geometry, meaning must therefore be fixed, static, or
mind-independent in the way a physical landscape is. The
opposite is closer to the chapter's actual claim: the meaning
manifold is a site of constant dynamics --- drift, damage,
repair, attractor formation --- and its geometry is precisely
what makes those dynamics describable rather than merely
observed.

A third misreading supposes that assigning meanings a formal
metric eliminates the role of context, use, and convention
that the Wittgensteinian tradition emphasises. It does not.
Context and use are part of what fixes a meaning's location
on the manifold at a given time; the geometry describes the
space those located meanings occupy and how they relate to
one another, not a replacement for the social processes that
place them there in the first place.

\begin{definition}{Semantic Domain}{semantic-domain}
  A \emph{semantic domain} is a measurable space
  $(W, \mu_W)$ where:
  \begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $W$ is a set of \emph{possible referents}:

    \item $\mu_W$ is a \emph{semantic measure}
          reflecting the relative salience or

  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

  A \emph{meaning manifold} $\mathcal{M}_L$ for a

  $(\mathcal{M}, g_S)$ where:
  \begin{itemize}[nosep]

    \item The metric $g_S$ is the \emph{semantic metric},

          meanings.
    \item The scalar curvature $\kappa_S$ of

  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The Semantic Metric


\begin{definition}{Semantic Distance}{sem-distance}
  The \emph{semantic distance} between meanings
  $m_1, m_2 \in \mathcal{M}$ is
  \[
    d_S(m_1, m_2)
    \;=\;
    \sqrt{
      D_\mathrm{KL}(m_1 \| m_2)
      + D_\mathrm{KL}(m_2 \| m_1)
    },
  \]
  the symmetrised KL-divergence between the
  probability distributions over $W$ induced by
  $m_1$ and $m_2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Meaning Distance Theorem}{meaning-distance}
  The semantic distance $d_S$ satisfies:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item $d_S(m_1, m_2) = 0$ iff $m_1$ and $m_2$
          induce identical partitions of $W$.
    \item $d_S$ is symmetric.
    \item $d_S$ satisfies a weak triangle inequality:
          $d_S(m_1, m_3) \le d_S(m_1, m_2)
          + d_S(m_2, m_3) + O(\epsilon^2)$ for
          $\epsilon$-close meanings.
    \item Semantic distance is non-decreasing under
          lossy projection: if $\pi: W \to W'$ is a
          projection, $d_S(\pi(m_1), \pi(m_2))
          \le d_S(m_1, m_2)$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  (i) $D_\mathrm{KL}(m_1 \| m_2) = 0$ iff $m_1 = m_2$
  as distributions. KL-divergence vanishes in both
  directions iff the distributions are identical.
  Identical distributions over $W$ induce identical
  partitions (same cells with same probabilities).

  (ii) The symmetrised KL-divergence is symmetric
  by construction.

  (iii) The symmetrised KL-divergence satisfies a
  second-order triangle inequality in the sense of
  a Fisher--Rao metric on the statistical manifold;
  for nearby points the approximation is exact.

  (iv) Projection coarsens partitions
  (Compression Theorem, \cref{thm:compression}).
  Coarser partitions have lower distinction capacity
  (\cref{thm:refinement-capacity}).
  Lower distinction capacity means fewer
  distinguishable referents, so
  $D_\mathrm{KL}(\pi(m_1) \| \pi(m_2))
  \le D_\mathrm{KL}(m_1 \| m_2)$,
  giving $d_S(\pi(m_1), \pi(m_2)) \le d_S(m_1, m_2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Synonymy and homonymy}{synonymy}
  The Meaning Distance Theorem makes synonymy and
  homonymy precise.
  \emph{Synonyms} are expressions with $d_S \approx 0$:
  they induce nearly identical partitions of $W$.
  \emph{Homonyms} are expressions whose surface form
  is identical but whose meaning manifold positions are
  far apart: $d_S(m_1, m_2) \gg 0$ despite sharing
  a lexical label.
  The theorem also formalises semantic bleaching
  (historical shift toward $d_S = 0$ between an original
  and extended meaning) and semantic drift
  (gradual movement through the meaning manifold).
\end{remark}


Semantic Curvature


The meaning manifold is not flat. Different regions
support different densities of semantic distinction.
The curvature of $(\mathcal{M}, g_S)$ encodes this
structure.

\begin{definition}{Semantic Curvature}{sem-curvature}
  The \emph{semantic curvature} at $m \in \mathcal{M}$
  is the scalar curvature $\kappa_S(m)$ of the
  Riemannian manifold $(\mathcal{M}, g_S)$.
  Positive curvature indicates a region of high
  semantic density: many distinct meanings are close
  together.
  Negative curvature indicates a region of low semantic
  density: meanings are sparsely distributed.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Semantic Curvature Theorem}{sem-curvature-thm}
  High semantic curvature at $m$ implies:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item \emph{Distinction sensitivity}: small
          semantic displacements produce large changes
          in the induced partition of $W$.
    \item \emph{Repair difficulty}: semantic damage
          at $m$ is harder to repair because many
          nearby meanings may be conflated.
    \item \emph{Admissibility concentration}: the
          admissibility curvature $\kappa_\adm$
          (\cref{thm:adm-curv}) is high at $m$,
          marking it as a semantic decision point
          where trajectory choices have large
          consequences.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  (i) In a region of high curvature, geodesic
  deviation is large: two nearby meanings diverge
  rapidly under parallel transport. Small displacements
  therefore produce large changes in $d_S$ from other
  fixed meanings, implying large changes in partition
  structure.

  (ii) Repair requires identifying and reconstructing
  the target meaning $m^*$
  (Definition~\ref{defn:repair-op}).
  In high-curvature regions, many candidate meanings
  are close to $m^*$ in the ambient space but induce
  very different partitions. The repair operator must
  distinguish among these candidates, which requires
  higher recoverability and more precise reconstruction
  information.

  (iii) By the Admissibility Curvature Theorem
  (\cref{thm:adm-curv}), admissibility curvature
  amplifies the sensitivity of future possibility to
  trajectory perturbations. In semantic space, high
  curvature marks meanings whose adoption or revision
  propagates large changes through the conceptual
  dependency structure $\mathcal{L}_S$.
\end{proof}

Conceptual Attractors


Meanings do not evolve freely. They cluster around
attractors: stable configurations that resist small
perturbations and attract nearby meanings.

\begin{definition}{Conceptual Attractor}{conceptual-attractor}
  A meaning $m^* \in \mathcal{M}$ is a
  \emph{conceptual attractor} if there exists a
  neighbourhood $U \ni m^*$ such that all meanings
  $m \in U$ evolve toward $m^*$ under the semantic
  dynamics:
  \[
    \dot{m} = -\nabla_m E_S(m),
  \]
  where $E_S : \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the
  \emph{semantic energy} measuring the cost of
  maintaining the distinction structure induced by $m$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Conceptual Attractor Theorem}{conceptual-attractor-thm}
  Every stable conceptual attractor $m^*$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item Minimises semantic energy in its basin of
          attraction:
          $E_S(m^*) \le E_S(m)$ for all $m$ in the basin.
    \item Is a local minimum of the distinction cost
          function $C$ (Definition~\ref{defn:dist-cost}):
          maintaining $m^*$ requires less cognitive,
          social, or computational cost than nearby
          alternatives.
    \item Has positive recoverability:
          $\reco(m^*, t) > 0$, since the attractor
          is maintained by ongoing semantic repair.
    \item Corresponds to a low-entropy region of the
          meaning manifold: $S(m^*, t) < \theta$
          for some threshold $\theta$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  (i) An attractor is a stable fixed point of
  $\dot{m} = -\nabla E_S(m)$.
  Stable fixed points occur at local minima of $E_S$.

  (ii) Semantic energy includes the cost of maintaining
  the partition structure of $m$.
  By the Distinction Cost Theorem
  (\cref{thm:refinement-cost}), maintaining finer
  distinctions costs more than coarser ones.
  A stable attractor balances distinction capacity
  against maintenance cost, settling at a local
  minimum of $C$.

  (iii) A conceptual attractor is actively maintained
  by the linguistic community through use, correction,
  and teaching — all instances of semantic repair.
  This maintenance keeps $\reco(m^*, t) > 0$.

  (iv) Low semantic energy corresponds to low
  multiplicity beneath the distinction structure,
  i.e.\ low entropy $S(m^*)$.
  High-entropy meanings are unstable: they conflate
  many referents, creating pressure toward either
  finer distinction (attractor approach) or complete
  merger (attractor collapse).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}{Basic-Level Categories}{basic-level}
  \bioicon\csicon
  In cognitive linguistics, \emph{basic-level categories}
  — dog, chair, tree — are learned first, named most
  easily, and used most frequently across cultures.
  The Conceptual Attractor Theorem predicts this:
  basic-level categories are conceptual attractors that
  minimise semantic energy by balancing distinction

Meaning Repair


Meanings degrade. Technical terms acquire informal
meanings through metaphorical extension. Evaluative
terms reverse their valence through ironic use.
Scientific concepts lose precision through
popularisation. Political terms are emptied through
euphemism. All of these are forms of semantic damage
requiring repair.

\begin{theorem}{Meaning Repair Theorem}{meaning-repair}
  Semantic repair — restoring a degraded meaning to
  its target distinction structure — is possible iff
  $\reco(m, t) > 0$.
  Among all admissible semantic repairs achieving
  $d_S(m_\mathrm{rep}, m^*) \le \epsilon$,
  the minimal-cost repair:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item Restores the partition of $W$ induced by $m^*$
          as closely as possible.
    \item Does not introduce new semantic distinctions
          not present in $m^*$.
    \item Preserves all non-damaged semantic relations
          in the neighbourhood of $m$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Repair Existence Theorem
  (\cref{thm:repair-exist}), repair is possible iff
  $\reco(m,t) > 0$.
  The minimal repair is given by the Minimal Repair
  Theorem (\cref{thm:minimal-repair}), which in the
  semantic setting corresponds to the minimal
  modification to the partition structure of $W$
  that moves $m$ within $\epsilon$ of $m^*$ in $d_S$.
  (i) follows from the definition of $d_S$: minimising
  $d_S(m_\mathrm{rep}, m^*)$ is equivalent to
  restoring the partition.
  (ii) follows from admissibility: introducing new
  distinctions not in $m^*$ would increase
  the repair cost beyond what is required.
  (iii) follows from the Repair Conservation Law
  (\cref{thm:repair-conservation}): admissible repair
  stays within the connected component of the
  recoverability manifold, preserving non-damaged
  semantic relations.
\end{proof}

  {popularisation}
  The word \emph{theory} has a precise scientific
  meaning (a well-confirmed explanatory framework
  with empirical support) and a degraded popular
  meaning (a conjecture or guess).
  This is semantic damage: the popular use has moved
  the meaning far from the scientific attractor in
  the meaning manifold.

  The Meaning Repair Theorem prescribes:
  (i) restore the partition: \emph{theory} should
  separate well-confirmed frameworks from guesses;
  (ii) do not introduce new distinctions: do not
  redefine \emph{theory} as \emph{law} or introduce
  additional conditions not in the original meaning;
  (iii) preserve non-damaged relations: the relationship
  between \emph{theory}, \emph{hypothesis}, and
  \emph{law} in the scientific vocabulary should be
  maintained.
  Science communication that achieves (i)--(iii) is
  performing admissible semantic repair.
\end{example}


Semantic Trajectories and Admissibility


Meanings evolve through time. A semantic trajectory
$\gamma_S : [t_0, T] \to \mathcal{M}$ describes the
evolution of a meaning through the meaning manifold.
The admissibility framework applies directly.

\begin{definition}{Semantic Reachability Volume}{sem-reach-vol}
  The \emph{semantic reachability volume} at meaning $m$,
  time $t$, is
  \[
    V_M(m, t)
    = \mu_\mathcal{M}\!\bigl(\reach_\mathcal{M}(m, t)\bigr),
  \]
  the measure of meanings accessible from $m$ through
  admissible semantic evolution.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Semantic Horizon Propagation Theorem}
  {sem-horizon-prop}
  When a meaning $m$ crosses the semantic horizon —
  when $\reco(m, t) \to 0$ — it propagates horizon
  effects to dependent meanings:
  for all $m'$ with $m \prec m'$
  (Definition~\ref{defn:dist-dep}),
  \[
    \reco(m', t)
    \;\le\;
    \reco(m', t \mid m)
    \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{\reco(m,t)>0\}}.
  \]
  The loss of a foundational meaning reduces the
  recoverability of all meanings that depend on it.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Distinction Dependency Theorem
  (\cref{thm:dist-dependency}), if $m \prec m'$,
  then loss of recoverability of $m$ reduces the
  recoverability of $m'$.
  When $\reco(m, t) = 0$, information required for
  the reconstruction of $m'$ via $m$ is unavailable.
  The indicator $\mathbf{1}_{\{\reco(m,t)>0\}}$
  captures the complete dependence: if the supporting
  meaning has zero recoverability, the recoverability
  of the dependent meaning is at most
  $\reco(m', t \mid m)$, but without $m$ this
  conditional recoverability may itself be zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Conceptual erosion cascades}{concept-cascade}
  The Semantic Horizon Propagation Theorem describes
  conceptual erosion cascades.
  When a foundational concept loses recoverability,
  the entire network of meanings that depends on it
  becomes harder to maintain.
  Historical examples abound:
  the erosion of the concept of \emph{evidence}
  in public discourse reduces the recoverability of
  \emph{scientific consensus}, \emph{expert opinion},
  and \emph{empirical fact}.
  The loss of the concept of \emph{conflict of interest}
  reduces the recoverability of \emph{impartial
  deliberation} and \emph{professional ethics}.
  These are not merely social problems but structural
  reachability failures in the collective meaning manifold.
\end{remark}


RSVP and the Meaning Manifold


The scalar-vector-entropy triple of RSVP
(\cref{ch:rsvp}) has a natural semantic interpretation.

\begin{proposition}{RSVP-Semantic Correspondence}{rsvp-semantic}
  Under the identification:
  \begin{align*}
    \Phi(x,t) &\;\leftrightarrow\;
    \text{semantic density at region }x\text{ of }W, \\
    \mathbf{v}(x,t) &\;\leftrightarrow\;
    \text{direction of semantic flow in }\mathcal{M}, \\
    S(x,t) &\;\leftrightarrow\;
    \text{semantic ambiguity (entropy of the partition)},
  \end{align*}
  the RSVP field equations (\cref{ch:rsvp}) describe
  the dynamics of a meaning manifold under linguistic
  use, repair, and degradation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  The scalar field $\Phi$ measures local distinction
  capacity in the semantic domain $W$.
  High $\Phi$ corresponds to densely structured
  regions of meaning where many fine distinctions are
  maintained.
  The vector field $\mathbf{v}$ describes the
  flow of semantic influence: how meanings at one
  region of $\mathcal{M}$ influence meanings at
  neighbouring regions through use, metaphor, and
  borrowing.
  The entropy field $S$ measures semantic ambiguity:
  the multiplicity of referents consistent with a
  given expression, corresponding to the hidden
  multiplicity beneath the partition induced by $m$.

  Under these identifications, the RSVP continuity
  equation (\cref{thm:capacity-conservation}) becomes
  a conservation law for semantic density, the
  constraint accumulation equation describes the
  growth of semantic ambiguity, and lamphrodyne
  relaxation (\cref{thm:lamphrodyne}) describes
  the tendency of linguistic communities to reduce
  ambiguity by converging toward semantic attractors.
\end{proof}


Semantic Geometry and Cognitive Realization


The meaning manifold is the cognitive substrate in
which the distinction ecology of Part~I is realised.
Chapter~\ref{ch:consciousness} showed that
consciousness is recursive repair of self-distinction
structures. Chapter~\ref{ch:preference-fields} showed
that preferences are gradients over admissible future
volume in state space.

Semantic geometry completes the cognitive picture by
providing the space within which these processes occur.
Consciousness maintains the meaning manifold through
recursive repair. Preferences flow along semantic
gradients. Intelligence is repair capacity over
the meaning manifold. The ecology of distinctions
is, at the cognitive scale, an ecology of meanings.

\begin{theorem}{Semantic Intelligence Theorem}{sem-intel}
  Cognitive intelligence $\mathcal{I}(\Sigma)$
  (\cref{defn:intelligence}) is proportional to
  the system's repair capacity over its meaning
  manifold $\mathcal{M}_\Sigma$:
  \[
    \mathcal{I}(\Sigma)
    \;\propto\;
    \kappa(\Sigma, \mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}_\Sigma})
    \cdot
    \kappa(\Sigma, \mathcal{D}_{\repair_\mathcal{M}}),
  \]
  where $\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{M}_\Sigma}$ is the
  distinction space of the meaning manifold and
  $\mathcal{D}_{\repair_\mathcal{M}}$ is the
  distinction space of the semantic repair processes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Intelligence--Repair Equivalence Theorem
  (\cref{thm:intel-repair}), intelligence is repair
  capacity over the system's own distinction space,
  including its repair processes.
  For a cognitive system, the primary distinction space
  is the meaning manifold $\mathcal{M}_\Sigma$:
  the distinctions the system maintains and can repair.
  Meta-intelligence — the ability to repair semantic
  repair processes — corresponds to
  $\kappa(\Sigma, \mathcal{D}_{\repair_\mathcal{M}})$.
  The product gives cognitive intelligence.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Language as distinction ecology infrastructure}
  {language-infra}
  The Semantic Intelligence Theorem explains why language
  is not merely a communication tool but a cognitive
  infrastructure.
  A system operating without language must maintain its
  meaning manifold $\mathcal{M}_\Sigma$ entirely through
  perceptual and motoric distinction structures.
  A system with language can offload meaning maintenance
  onto the shared linguistic meaning manifold of its
  community, dramatically extending its effective
  $\kappa(\Sigma, \mathcal{D}_\mathcal{M})$ at low cost.
  Language is therefore a distributed repair system for
  the collective meaning manifold.
\end{remark}


Related Frameworks

\label{sec:ch20-related-frameworks}

\subsection*{Information Geometry: The Uniqueness of the Metric}

\emph{Convergence.} Information geometry's central technical
result is that spaces of probability distributions possess
essentially one metric --- the Fisher information metric, unique
up to scale --- invariant under sufficient statistics and
respecting the distinguishability of nearby distributions
\parencite{amari2016,ay2017}. Where meanings are represented
distributionally, as is standard in computational semantics, the
Fisher-Rao metric is a natural, principled candidate for exactly
the kind of semantic distance this chapter's Semantic Distance
(\cref{defn:sem-distance}) formalises.

\emph{Divergence.} The Fisher-Rao metric is derived from a fixed
family of distributions together with an invariance requirement
under sufficient statistics --- a narrow, specific justification.
This chapter's Semantic Curvature (\cref{defn:sem-curvature}) is
derived instead from repair structure: the Semantic Curvature
Theorem (\cref{thm:sem-curvature-thm}) ties curvature to how
difficult a meaning is to repair under perturbation, with no
appeal to statistical invariance at all. The two metrics could in
principle coincide for a particular choice of distributional
semantic model, but they are justified by entirely different
arguments, and nothing in this chapter establishes that they must
agree in general.

\emph{Open question.} Whether repair-based semantic curvature and
Fisher-Rao curvature can be shown to coincide under some natural
correspondence --- identifying repair difficulty with statistical
distinguishability, for instance --- or whether they are
genuinely different quantities that happen to share the name
``curvature,'' is not settled here.

\subsection*{Crutchfield: Concepts as Causal States}

\emph{Convergence.} Chapters~\ref{ch:history} and~\ref{ch:memory}
engaged computational mechanics on history compression and
memory; this chapter's concern is closer to its treatment of
causal states as stable categories. Causal states are equivalence
classes of histories that collapse together because they are
predictively indistinguishable, and a process's full set of
causal states is the minimal representation needed to predict its
future as well as its complete history would
\parencite{crutchfield2012}. This chapter's Conceptual Attractors
(\cref{defn:conceptual-attractor}) read naturally as exactly this
construction applied to a meaning manifold: a concept is a stable
region of semantic space toward which many distinct experiential
histories converge, because, for the purposes of future
categorisation, those histories are equivalent.

\emph{Divergence.} Causal states are defined purely in terms of
predictive sufficiency for an external observer modelling a
process from outside. The Conceptual Attractor Theorem and
Meaning Repair Theorem
(\cref{thm:conceptual-attractor-thm,thm:meaning-repair}) instead
characterise concepts by stability \emph{under repair}: a
conceptual attractor is one the system's own repair processes can
restore after perturbation, a fact about the system's relation to
its own concept, not merely about prediction accuracy for an
outside observer. A predictively minimal causal state could in
principle be fragile under perturbation, requiring no inherent
stability, while this chapter's conceptual attractors are
specifically the configurations a system is disposed to actively
defend.

\emph{Open question.} Whether every causal state of a
sufficiently rich predictive process is automatically a
conceptual attractor in this chapter's stronger, repair-stability
sense, or whether predictive sufficiency and repair-stability can
come apart --- yielding causal states that are not concepts, or
concepts that are not causal states --- is not resolved here.

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\begin{chapsummary}
  \item Meanings are stable distinction structures over
        a semantic domain; the meaning manifold
        $(\mathcal{M}, g_S)$ is their geometric home.
  \item Semantic distance measures informational
        divergence between partition structures;
        projection reduces it
        (\cref{thm:meaning-distance}).
  \item High semantic curvature marks regions where
        small displacements produce large partition
        changes, repair is difficult, and semantic
        decisions have disproportionate consequences
        (\cref{thm:sem-curvature-thm}).
  \item Conceptual attractors are low-energy, low-cost,
        low-entropy, high-recoverability meanings that
        resist small perturbations
        (\cref{thm:conceptual-attractor-thm}).
  \item Semantic repair is possible iff
        $\reco(m,t) > 0$; minimal repair
        restores partition structure without introducing
        new distinctions or destroying existing relations
        (\cref{thm:meaning-repair}).
  \item Loss of foundational meanings propagates
        horizon effects to all dependent meanings
        (\cref{thm:sem-horizon-prop}).
  \item RSVP fields $(\Phi, \mathbf{v}, S)$ describe
        semantic density, flow, and ambiguity
        (\cref{prop:rsvp-semantic}).
  \item Cognitive intelligence is semantic repair
        capacity over the meaning manifold
        (\cref{thm:sem-intel}).
\end{chapsummary}

Self-Reconstruction

\label{sec:ch21-self-reconstruction}

The safest route through this chapter is to avoid making
consciousness fundamental. Within the logic of this book,
consciousness should emerge as a special regime of
recursive reconstruction occurring within
distinction-preserving systems, rather than being
postulated as an irreducible property. The chapter's
machinery therefore derives consciousness from history,
recoverability, repair, and admissibility. The central
progression is
\[
  \text{Distinction} \to \text{History} \to \text{Reconstruction}
  \to \text{Self-Model} \to \text{Consciousness}.
\]
The first step is to define self-reference geometrically.


Why Integrated Information Is Not Enough

\label{sec:ch21-why-iit}

The most influential contemporary mathematical theory of
consciousness, Integrated Information Theory, proposes that
consciousness corresponds to a scalar quantity, $\Phi$,
measuring how much information a system's causal structure
generates as an integrated whole beyond what its parts
generate independently \parencite{tononi2015}. A system is
conscious to the degree that $\Phi$ is large, and the theory
offers, at least in principle, a procedure for computing
$\Phi$ from a system's causal architecture.

This is a serious proposal, and it shares with the present
chapter the conviction that consciousness should be
approached as a structural property of a system rather than
postulated as an irreducible extra ingredient
\parencite{dennett1991}. Where it differs is in treating
consciousness as a quantity rather than a regime. A
high-$\Phi$ system, on the integrated-information account, is
conscious in roughly the way a hot object has a high
temperature: more or less of one scalar property. The present
chapter's account is structurally different. Consciousness
here is not a quantity a system has more or less of in
isolation; it is a self-referential \emph{process} ---
recursive reconstruction operating on the system's own
history --- that either is or is not actively underway, and
which admits its own internal geometry (depth, reachability,
repair) rather than reducing to a single number. This is
closer in spirit to the ``hard problem'' framing's insistence
that something process-like, not merely quantitative, is at
stake \parencite{chalmers1996}, while declining to treat that
process as forever explanatorily inaccessible.

Chapter~\ref{ch:semantic-geometry} gave meaning a full
geometric structure: a manifold, a metric, curvature, repair.
This chapter asks what changes when a system does not merely
possess such a structure but models \emph{itself} as a point
within one --- when the meaning manifold's most important
referent becomes the system doing the modelling.


What Would Falsify This Realization

\label{sec:ch21-falsifiability}

The chapter's culminating claim, Consciousness as Recursive
Repair (\cref{thm:consciousness-recursive-repair}), asserts
that consciousness is a genuine instance of the repair
machinery of Chapter~\ref{ch:repair}, applied recursively to
a system's self-model, and not merely a process that
resembles repair by loose analogy. This is falsifiable
against a specific test: the theorem should be derivable from
the general repair apparatus (\cref{thm:repair-exist}) plus
the self-model construction defined below, with no
consciousness-specific axiom introduced along the way. If
establishing the theorem required smuggling in some
additional postulate --- something true of self-modelling
systems that is not already a consequence of repair theory
applied to a system's own history --- the claim that
consciousness realizes repair, rather than merely echoing its
vocabulary, would fail.

\section{Common Misinterpretations of Consciousness as
  Recursive Repair}
\label{sec:ch21-misinterpretations}

A first misreading takes this chapter to claim that it has
solved the hard problem of consciousness --- that recursive
repair explains why there is something it is like to be a
conscious system. It does not make this claim. The chapter
gives necessary structural conditions for the kind of process
that, in systems we already agree are conscious, accompanies
subjective experience; it remains silent on why that process
is accompanied by experience at all, which is the hard
problem in its sharpest form.

A second misreading assumes that any system performing
self-reconstruction must therefore be conscious --- that a
thermostat modelling its own temperature history qualifies.
The Depth Theorem and Conscious Reachability Theorem, proved
later in this chapter, place quantitative floors on
recursive depth and self-reachable volume; trivial
self-reference of the kind a thermostat performs falls far
short of them.

A third misreading conflates this chapter's separation of
intelligence and consciousness with a claim that the two are
unrelated or never co-occur. The Intelligence--Consciousness
Separation Theorem establishes logical independence, not
empirical dissociation: most systems encountered in practice,
biological or artificial, may well possess both or neither.
The theorem's content is that possessing one does not entail
possessing the other.


What This Chapter Establishes

\label{sec:ch21-preview}

By the end of this chapter, self-reference will have been
derived geometrically from history and reconstruction, the
Memory Requirement and Recursive Representation Theorems will
establish what self-modelling demands, and Consciousness as
Recursive Repair will identify consciousness with a specific
recursive regime of the repair machinery already developed.
Chapter~\ref{ch:preference-fields} then asks what a system
with this kind of self-model \emph{wants}: whether preference
itself can be derived from the same admissibility geometry,
rather than introduced as a separate axiomatic primitive.

\begin{definition}{System History}{system-history-ch21}
  Let $\Sigma$ be a system. Its \emph{history} is
  \[
    H_\Sigma = \{e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_n\}.
  \]
\end{definition}

The key object is not state. It is recoverability of state,
in the sense of Chapter~\ref{ch:recoverability}.

\begin{definition}{Self-Model}{self-model}
  A \emph{self-model} is a reconstruction operator
  \[
    M_\Sigma : H_\Sigma \to \hat{\Sigma}.
  \]
  \index{self-model|textbf}
\end{definition}

The system is building an estimate of itself from its own
history, using the reconstruction machinery of
\cref{thm:reconstruction}.

\begin{theorem}{Memory Requirement Theorem}{memory-consciousness}
  Conscious reconstruction requires nonzero recoverable
  history.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Suppose $H_\Sigma = \varnothing$. Then $M_\Sigma$ has no
  input, so $\hat{\Sigma}$ cannot depend upon previous
  states, and no temporal self-reconstruction is possible.
  Therefore consciousness, understood as self-reconstruction,
  requires $|H_\Sigma| > 0$.
\end{proof}

This connects directly to the memory and recoverability
machinery of Chapters~\ref{ch:recoverability}--\ref{ch:memory}.


Recursive Self-Modeling

\label{sec:ch21-recursive}

\begin{definition}{Recursive Self-Model}{recursive-self-model}
  A \emph{recursive self-model} satisfies
  \[
    M_\Sigma = M\bigl(M(H_\Sigma)\bigr).
  \]
  \index{self-model!recursive|textbf}
\end{definition}

The system does not merely model itself. It models itself
modeling itself.

\begin{theorem}{Recursive Representation Theorem}{recursive-self}
  If $M_\Sigma = M(M(H_\Sigma))$, then self-reference
  becomes representable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The first reconstruction produces $\hat{\Sigma}_1 = M(H_\Sigma)$.
  The second reconstruction acts on $\hat{\Sigma}_1$, giving
  $\hat{\Sigma}_2 = M(\hat{\Sigma}_1)$. The representation
  $\hat{\Sigma}_2$ therefore contains information about the
  representation $\hat{\Sigma}_1$ itself. This establishes
  self-reference within the formal machinery of
  reconstruction operators.
\end{proof}

This is the first point at which consciousness, in the
sense developed here, begins to appear: not as a postulated
substance but as a structural consequence of applying a
reconstruction operator to its own output.


Awareness

\label{sec:ch21-awareness}

Recall the Hearing Your Name example from
Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction}: a crowded room full of ignored
sound reorganises entirely around a single activated
distinction, with nothing physically new having entered the
room. That example was offered there as a phenomenon, with no
machinery yet available to explain it. The machinery is now
available. What changed in that room was not the acoustic
signal but the system's model of itself as a relevant target
within it --- precisely the kind of self-referential update
the awareness functional below is built to measure.

\begin{definition}{Awareness Functional}{awareness-functional}
  Let $D(\Sigma, \hat{\Sigma})$ denote reconstruction error.
  Define
  \[
    A = -\frac{d}{dt} D(\Sigma, \hat{\Sigma}).
  \]
  \index{awareness|textbf}
\end{definition}

Awareness is the \emph{improvement} of self-reconstruction,
not the mere possession of a self-model.

\begin{theorem}{Awareness Theorem}{awareness}
  A system is aware to the extent that reconstruction error
  decreases.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  If $\frac{d}{dt}D < 0$, the model becomes more accurate
  over time. If $\frac{d}{dt}D = 0$, the system gains no new
  self-information. Therefore awareness, as defined by $A$,
  is proportional to the negative rate of growth of
  reconstruction error.
\end{proof}


Intelligence and Consciousness Are Independent

\label{sec:ch21-separation}

The next result distinguishes intelligence from
consciousness; this chapter does not identify them.

\begin{theorem}{Intelligence--Consciousness Separation}{intel-consciousness}
  Intelligence does not imply consciousness.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Intelligence--Repair Equivalence
  (\cref{thm:intel-repair}, Chapter~\ref{ch:repair-intelligence}),
  intelligence is repair capability. Let $\Sigma_I$ be
  highly effective at repair, and suppose $M_{\Sigma_I}$ is
  absent. Then repair occurs without self-reconstruction.
  Hence intelligence may exist without consciousness.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}{Consciousness Without Intelligence}{consciousness-without-intelligence}
  Consciousness does not imply strong repair capability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  A system may possess recursive self-models while remaining
  ineffective at solving external problems. Thus
  $M_\Sigma \ne 0$ does not imply high repair capacity
  $\mathcal{I}(\Sigma)$ in the sense of
  \cref{thm:intel-repair}.
\end{proof}
