Optimal Transport


Many problems in mathematics concern comparison. Given
two probability distributions, two geometric structures,
two semantic states, or two ecological configurations,
one may ask how different they are.

Optimal transport addresses a stronger question.

Rather than merely measuring difference, it asks:

\emph{What is the least costly transformation that converts
one distribution into another?}

Originally introduced by Monge in 1781 as a problem of
moving soil between locations, optimal transport has
become one of the central mathematical theories connecting
geometry, probability, information theory, economics,
machine learning, and dynamical systems.

Within the present framework optimal transport provides a
natural language for repair. Damage corresponds to
displacement within a distinction space. Repair
corresponds to the transport of the damaged state toward a
recoverable configuration. Regeneration corresponds to
preservation of the capacity to perform such transport.

Large-Deviation Theory


Many of the systems studied throughout this book possess
an apparent paradox. They spend most of their time near
stable states and yet occasionally undergo dramatic
reorganizations. Ecosystems collapse. Scientific
paradigms shift. Institutions fail. Semantic structures
reorganize. Admissible regions suddenly contract.

Such transitions are often too rare to be explained by
ordinary perturbation theory, yet too important to be
ignored.

Large-deviation theory provides a mathematical framework
for understanding these events. Rather than studying
typical behavior, it studies exponentially improbable
behavior. It asks how unlikely transitions occur, what
paths they follow, and how their probabilities scale with
system size.

Within the present framework large-deviation theory
provides the mathematical language of collapse,
regeneration, boundary crossing, and admissibility
transitions.

is the cumulant generating function.
\end{theorem}

This theorem establishes one of the foundational results
of the subject.


Rate Functions as Geometric Potentials


The rate function may be interpreted geometrically.

States with low

\[
I(x)
\]

occupy valleys.

States with high

\[
I(x)
\]

occupy peaks.

Typical trajectories descend toward minima of

\[
I.
\]

Rare transitions require climbing effective barriers.

This interpretation closely parallels the admissibility
potential introduced in earlier sections.

\begin{rem}
Many admissibility phenomena can be understood as motion
through a large-deviation landscape.
\end{rem}


Path Space Large Deviations


Many systems are better described by trajectories than by
states.

Let

\[
\gamma(t)
\]

denote a trajectory.

The probability of observing a particular trajectory often
takes the asymptotic form

\[
P[\gamma]
\sim
e^{-S[\gamma]}.
\]

The functional

\[
S[\gamma]
\]

is called the action.

\begin{definition}{Rate Functional}{rate-functional}
A rate functional assigns an exponential cost to an
entire trajectory:

\[
S:
\Gamma
\rightarrow
[0,\infty].
\]
\end{definition}

The most probable trajectory minimizes

\[
S[\gamma].
\]

This principle is the probabilistic analogue of the
principle of least action in physics.


Freidlin--Wentzell Theory


Consider a stochastic differential equation

\[
dx_t
=
f(x_t),dt
+
\sqrt{\varepsilon},dW_t.
\]

The deterministic dynamics are governed by

\[
f(x).
\]

The parameter

\[
\varepsilon
\]

controls noise strength.

As

\[
\varepsilon
\rightarrow0,
\]

the probability of observing trajectory

\[
\gamma
\]

satisfies

\[
P[\gamma]
\sim
\exp
\left(
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
S[\gamma]
\right).
\]

The Freidlin--Wentzell action is

\[
S[\gamma]
=
\frac12
\int
|
\dot\gamma-f(\gamma)
|^2
,dt.
\]

\begin{theorem}
The most probable noise-induced transition minimizes the
Freidlin--Wentzell action.
\end{theorem}

This theorem provides a rigorous description of rare
escape events.


Metastability


Many systems spend long periods near stable states before
undergoing sudden transitions.

\begin{definition}{Metastable State}{metastable-state}
A metastable state is locally stable but globally
nonoptimal.
\end{definition}

Examples include:

\[
\text{supercooled liquids},
\]

\[
\text{ecological equilibria},
\]

\[
\text{scientific paradigms},
\]

\[
\text{bureaucratic structures},
\]

and

\[
\text{semantic conventions}.
\]

Large-deviation theory explains why such states persist
for long periods despite not being globally optimal.

\begin{prop}
Escape times from metastable states scale exponentially.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Escape requires crossing a positive action barrier

\[
\Delta S.
\]

Large-deviation theory yields

\[
\tau
\sim
e^{\Delta S/\varepsilon}.
\]

Therefore escape times grow exponentially as noise
decreases.
\end{proof}


Distinction Collapse as a Rare Event


Let

\[
D(t)
\]

denote distinction capacity.

Suppose repair maintains a positive equilibrium

\[
D^*.
\]

Collapse to

\[
D=0
\]

requires a sequence of atypical fluctuations.

The probability of collapse therefore satisfies

\[
P(D\rightarrow0)
\sim
e^{-I_{\mathrm{collapse}}}.
\]

\begin{definition}{Collapse Action}{collapse-action}
The collapse action is the minimum rate functional
required to drive a system from a viable distinction state
to a collapsed state.
\end{definition}

Collapse is therefore not merely a state.

It is a trajectory possessing a geometric cost.


Repair and Action Minimization


Suppose

\[
\gamma_D
\]

is a damaged trajectory and

\[
\gamma_R
\]

a repaired trajectory.

Define repair action

\[
S_R
=
\int
L_R(\gamma,\dot\gamma)
,dt.
\]

\begin{definition}{Optimal Repair Path}{optimal-repair}
An optimal repair path minimizes

\[
S_R.
\]
\end{definition}

This construction links repair theory to variational
principles.

Repair becomes a path-selection problem.


Admissibility Transitions


One of the most important applications of large-deviation
theory in the present framework concerns admissibility
collapse.

Let

\[
\mathcal A
\]

denote an admissible region.

A trajectory initially satisfies

\[
\gamma(t)
\in
\mathcal A.
\]

Rare fluctuations may eventually drive the trajectory
across

\[
\partial\mathcal A.
\]

The probability of such a transition satisfies

\[
P
\left(
\gamma
\text{ exits }
\mathcal A
\right)
\sim
e^{-I_A}.
\]

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Barrier}{adm-barrier}
The admissibility barrier is the minimum action required
to reach

\[
\partial\mathcal A.
\]
\end{definition}

Systems possessing large admissibility barriers are
robust.

Systems possessing small barriers are fragile.


Quasipotentials


For stochastic systems one may define a quasipotential

\[
V(x).
\]

The quasipotential measures the minimum action required to
reach state

\[
x.
\]

\begin{definition}{Quasipotential}{quasipotential}
The quasipotential is

\[
V(x)
=
\inf_{\gamma}
S[\gamma].
\]
\end{definition}

The quasipotential plays a role analogous to an energy
landscape.

Stable states correspond to minima.

Collapse states correspond to distant basins separated by
large barriers.


Reachability and Large Deviations


Reachability theory studies which states can be attained.

Large-deviation theory studies how difficult those states
are to attain.

Combining the two yields a richer notion of future
possibility.

\begin{definition}{Reachability Cost}{reachability-cost}
The reachability cost of state

\[
x
\]

is

\[
C_R(x)
=
I(x).
\]
\end{definition}

Reachable states therefore possess differing degrees of
accessibility.

Two futures may both be reachable while differing by many
orders of magnitude in probability.


Generative Admissibility


Let

\[
V_A(t)
\]

denote admissible volume.

Generative systems satisfy

\[
\frac{dV_A}{dt}
\ge0.
\]

Large-deviation theory provides a complementary
perspective.

Rather than examining average volume growth, one examines
the action required to destroy admissibility.

\begin{definition}{Generative Robustness}{generative-robustness}
The generative robustness of a system is

\[
G
=

\inf
S[\gamma_{\mathrm{collapse}}],
\]

the minimum action required to force admissibility
collapse.
\end{definition}

Large values indicate strongly regenerative systems.

Small values indicate fragile systems.


Summary


Large-deviation theory studies exponentially rare events,
action functionals, metastability, and transition
probabilities. Rate functions measure the cost of
atypical states, action functionals measure the cost of
atypical trajectories, and quasipotentials describe the
global structure of possibility landscapes.

Within the present framework distinction collapse,
scientific revolutions, semantic shifts, repair
trajectories, ecological transitions, and admissibility
boundary crossings may all be interpreted as
large-deviation phenomena. The most probable transition is
the trajectory minimizing action, while robustness is
measured by the action barrier protecting a system from
collapse.

Large-deviation theory therefore provides the
mathematical language of rare but consequential events,
complementing the dynamical and geometric methods
developed throughout the text.


Geometric Reachability


The central concern of this book is not merely what a
system is, but what a system can become.

Two systems may possess identical present states while
differing radically in their accessible futures. A seed
and a pebble may occupy comparable volumes. A healthy
institution and a failing institution may possess similar
budgets. A living civilization and a dying civilization
may possess comparable resources.

The difference lies not in their current state but in
their future possibility structure.

Reachability theory provides a mathematical framework for
describing this distinction.

Throughout Chapters~13--31 reachability volume serves as
one of the principal quantities used to characterize
repair, regeneration, intelligence, governance,
admissibility, and future preservation.


Control Systems


Let

\[
\dot x
=

f(x,u)
\]

be a controlled dynamical system.

Here

\[
x(t)\in X
\]

denotes the state of the system and

\[
u(t)\in U
\]

denotes a control input.

The function

\[
f
\]

determines the local dynamics.

Unlike autonomous systems, controlled systems possess
multiple possible futures.

The control variable selects among them.

\begin{definition}{Admissible Control}{adm-control}
A control function

\[
u(t)
\]

is admissible if it satisfies all imposed constraints.
\end{definition}

Examples include physical force limitations, resource
constraints, informational constraints, ecological
constraints, and admissibility constraints.


Reachable Sets


\begin{definition}{Reachable Set}{reachable-set}
Let

\[
x_0
\]

be an initial state.

The reachable set at time (t) is

\[
R_t(x_0)
=

\left{
x(t)
:
u(\tau)
\text{ admissible}
\right}.
\]
\end{definition}

The reachable set contains every state that can be
attained from the initial condition within time (t).

Reachability therefore converts dynamics into geometry.

Instead of asking what trajectory occurs, we ask what
region of possibility remains accessible.

\begin{ex}
For

\[
\dot x=u,
\qquad
|u|\le1,
\]

the reachable set is

\[
R_t
=

[-t,t].
\]

Reachability grows linearly with time.
\end{ex}


Reachability Volume


The size of a reachable region is measured by its volume.

\begin{definition}{Reachability Volume}{reachability-volume}
Let

\[
\mu
\]

be a measure on the state space.

The reachability volume is

\[
V_R(t)
=

\mu(R_t).
\]
\end{definition}

Reachability volume measures future flexibility.

Large values indicate many accessible futures.

Small values indicate restricted possibility.

\begin{rem}
Throughout this text reachability volume plays a role
analogous to phase-space volume in statistical mechanics.
\end{rem}


Monotonicity of Reachability


Reachable sets frequently expand through time.

\begin{theorem}{Reachability Monotonicity}{reachability-monotonicity}
Suppose controls remain available throughout the interval

\[
[0,t_2].
\]

Then

\[
R_{t_1}
\subseteq
R_{t_2}
\]

for

\[
t_1<t_2.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Any trajectory achievable in time

\[
t_1
\]

can be followed by a period of zero control until

\[
t_2.
\]

Hence every state reachable at

\[
t_1
\]

remains reachable at

\[
t_2.
\]

Therefore

\[
R_{t_1}
\subseteq
R_{t_2}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
Reachability volume is nondecreasing:

\[
V_R(t_1)
\le
V_R(t_2).
\]
\end{corollary}

This result describes idealized systems.

Real systems often experience reachability loss due to
resource depletion, damage, lock-in effects, or
admissibility collapse.


Reachability Entropy


The raw volume of a reachable set depends upon units and
dimension.

A more useful quantity is the logarithm of volume.

\begin{definition}{Reachability Entropy}{reachability-entropy}
The reachability entropy is

\[
S_R
=

\log V_R.
\]
\end{definition}

Reachability entropy measures the effective number of
future possibilities.

\begin{prop}
If

\[
R_1
\]

and

\[
R_2
\]

are independent reachable regions, then

\[
S_R(R_1\times R_2)
=

S_R(R_1)
+
S_R(R_2).
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Volumes multiply:

\[
\Vol(R_1\times R_2)
=

\Vol(R_1)\Vol(R_2).
\]

Taking logarithms yields

\[
\log
\Vol(R_1\times R_2)
=

\log\Vol(R_1)
+
\log\Vol(R_2).
\]
\end{proof}

This additivity property mirrors the behavior of entropy
throughout statistical mechanics and information theory.


Reachability as Distinction Capacity


Reachability may also be interpreted as a distinction
measure.

Let

\[
\epsilon
\]

denote observational resolution.

The number of distinguishable future states is

\[
N_R
\approx
\frac{V_R}{\epsilon^n}.
\]

The corresponding distinction capacity is

\[
D_R
=

\log N_R.
\]

Substituting gives

\[
D_R
=

## \log V_R

n\log\epsilon.
\]

Hence

\[
D_R
=

S_R
+
\text{constant}.
\]

\begin{prop}
Reachability entropy and future distinction capacity are
equivalent up to scale.
\end{prop}

This result provides a direct bridge between reachability
theory and the distinction framework developed in earlier
chapters.


Boundary Structure


The topology of the reachable set often matters as much as
its size.

\begin{definition}{Reachability Boundary}{reachability-boundary}
The reachability boundary is

\[
\partial R_t.
\]
\end{definition}

Boundary points represent states that are barely
reachable.

Small perturbations may render them inaccessible.

\begin{definition}{Reachability Margin}{reachability-margin}
For state

\[
x\in R_t,
\]

the reachability margin is

\[
m(x)
=

d(x,\partial R_t).
\]
\end{definition}

Small margins indicate fragility.

Large margins indicate robust accessibility.


Boundary Collapse


One of the most important phenomena discussed throughout
the book is boundary collapse.

\begin{definition}{Boundary Collapse}{boundary-collapse}
Boundary collapse occurs when

\[
V_R(t)
\rightarrow0.
\]
\end{definition}

Equivalently,

\[
S_R
\rightarrow
-\infty.
\]

The system retains fewer and fewer future options until
only a negligible set remains.

\begin{theorem}{Reachability Collapse Criterion}{reachability-collapse}
If

\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}
V_R(t)
=

0,
\]

then

\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}
S_R
=

-\infty.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since

\[
S_R
=

\log V_R,
\]

the result follows from

\[
\log x
\rightarrow
-\infty
\]

as

\[
x\rightarrow0^+.
\]
\end{proof}

This simple observation underlies much of the geometric
analysis developed later in the text.

Collapse is often more accurately understood as
reachability contraction than as immediate failure.


Reachability and Intelligence


Throughout Chapters~16--18 intelligence is interpreted in
terms of reachability.

An intelligent system is not merely one that predicts.

It is one that expands accessible futures.

\begin{definition}{Reachability Intelligence}{reachability-intelligence}
The reachability intelligence of a system

\[
\Sigma
\]

is

\[
\mathcal I_R(\Sigma)
=

\frac{d}{dt}
V_R(\Sigma).
\]
\end{definition}

Positive values indicate future expansion.

Negative values indicate future contraction.

\begin{rem}
This definition generalizes many traditional notions of
adaptation, planning, and problem solving.
\end{rem}


Reachability and Repair


Repair can be interpreted geometrically as restoration of
lost reachability.

Suppose damage changes

\[
R_t
\]

to

\[
R_t'.
\]

The reachability deficit is

\[
\Delta_R
=

## V_R

V_R'.
\]

\begin{definition}{Repair Efficiency}{repair-efficiency}
Repair efficiency is

\[
\eta_R
=

\frac{\Delta_R^{\mathrm{restored}}}
{\Delta_R}.
\]
\end{definition}

Perfect repair satisfies

\[
\eta_R=1.
\]

Complete failure satisfies

\[
\eta_R=0.
\]


Reachability and Regeneration


Repair restores lost volume.

Regeneration restores the ability to generate volume.

Let

\[
G_R
=

\frac{d}{dt}
V_R.
\]

\begin{definition}{Regenerative Capacity}{regenerative-capacity}
The regenerative capacity is

\[
\kappa_R
=

\frac{dG_R}{dt}.
\]
\end{definition}

Positive values indicate accelerating expansion of future
possibility.

Negative values indicate declining future flexibility.

This distinction parallels the difference between repair
and regeneration introduced earlier.


Reachability and Admissibility


Reachability alone is insufficient.

Many futures may be reachable while remaining destructive,
pathological, or self-terminating.

Let

\[
\mathcal A
\]

denote the admissible region.

Define admissible reachability volume

\[
\Vol_A
=

\mu(R_t\cap\mathcal A).
\]

\begin{definition}{Admissible Reachability}{adm-reachability}
The admissible reachability entropy is

\[
S_A
=

\log\Vol_A.
\]
\end{definition}

This quantity measures not merely what can happen, but
what can happen while remaining viable.

Much of the later admissibility program can be viewed as
the study of

\[
\Vol_A
\]

rather than

\[
V_R.
\]


The Reachability Principle


One of the recurring themes of the text may be stated
formally.

\begin{theorem}{Reachability Principle}{reachability-principle}
A system possessing greater admissible reachability
contains a larger space of future distinctions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Admissible reachability volume determines the number of
accessible distinguishable future states.

Larger admissible volume therefore implies greater future
distinction capacity.
\end{proof}

This theorem forms one of the conceptual bridges between
distinction theory and admissibility geometry.


Summary


Reachability theory transforms dynamics into geometry.
Reachable sets describe accessible futures, reachability
volume measures future flexibility, reachability entropy
measures future distinction capacity, and boundary
collapse corresponds to contraction of possibility space.

Repair restores lost reachability. Regeneration restores
the ability to generate reachability. Intelligence
expands reachability. Admissibility restricts
reachability to viable futures.

For this reason reachability volume serves as one of the
central geometric quantities appearing throughout
Chapters~13--31 and provides much of the mathematical
foundation for the admissibility program.


Admissibility Geometry


The central claim of this book is that persistence alone
is not sufficient.

A crystal persists.

A bureaucracy persists.

A parasite persists.

A dictatorship persists.

A cancer persists.

Persistence therefore cannot serve as a universal measure
of value, viability, intelligence, or success.

The deeper question concerns the preservation of future
possibility.

Not every continuation preserves the conditions required
for further continuation.

A system may survive while progressively destroying the
space of futures available to itself and others.

This observation motivates the concept of admissibility.

Admissibility concerns the geometry of viable future
possibility.

The primary object of study is not a state, a trajectory,
or a utility function.

It is the structure of futures that remain available.


The Admissible Region


Let

\[
X
\]

denote a state space, trajectory space, semantic space,
or possibility space.

\begin{definition}{Admissible Region}{adm-region}
The admissible region is a subset

\[
\mathcal A
\subseteq
X
\]

consisting of all states satisfying the viability
conditions of the system.
\end{definition}

The precise definition of admissibility depends upon the
domain.

In ecology it may correspond to sustainability.

In engineering it may correspond to safety.

In governance it may correspond to preservation of future
optionality.

In scientific inquiry it may correspond to preservation
of revision and falsifiability.

The mathematical structure remains the same.

\begin{rem}
Admissibility is intentionally more general than
optimization.

Optimization asks whether a state is preferred.

Admissibility asks whether future preference remains
possible.
\end{rem}


Admissibility Volume


The simplest global quantity associated with an
admissible region is its measure.

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Volume}{adm-volume}
Let

\[
\mu
\]

be a measure on (X).

The admissibility volume is

\[
V_A
=

\mu(\mathcal A).
\]
\end{definition}

Admissibility volume measures the size of the viable
future region.

Large values correspond to broad flexibility.

Small values correspond to fragility.

Vanishing admissibility volume corresponds to collapse.

\begin{prop}
If

\[
\mathcal A_1
\subseteq
\mathcal A_2,
\]

then

\[
V_A(\mathcal A_1)
\le
V_A(\mathcal A_2).
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Monotonicity follows directly from the monotonicity of
measures.

If one set is contained within another, its measure
cannot exceed the measure of the larger set.
\end{proof}

This simple observation provides the basis for much of
the ordering structure used throughout later chapters.


Admissibility Entropy


Volume alone is often inconvenient because it depends upon
dimension and units.

As in statistical mechanics and reachability theory, it
is useful to introduce a logarithmic quantity.

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Entropy}{adm-entropy}
The admissibility entropy is

\[
S_A
=

\log V_A.
\]
\end{definition}

Admissibility entropy measures the effective number of
admissible futures.

\begin{prop}
For independent admissible systems

\[
\mathcal A_1
\]

and

\[
\mathcal A_2,
\]

\[
S_A(\mathcal A_1\times\mathcal A_2)
=

S_A(\mathcal A_1)
+
S_A(\mathcal A_2).
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Since volume is multiplicative,

\[
V_A(\mathcal A_1\times\mathcal A_2)
=

V_A(\mathcal A_1)
V_A(\mathcal A_2).
\]

Taking logarithms gives the result.
\end{proof}

This additive property makes admissibility entropy
analogous to thermodynamic entropy and reachability
entropy.


Local Admissibility Structure


Global volume is only part of the story.

The local geometry of admissibility is equally important.

Let

\[
x\in\mathcal A.
\]

Small perturbations may either preserve or destroy
admissibility.

To quantify this sensitivity we introduce an
admissibility potential.

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Potential}{adm-potential}
The admissibility potential is

\[
\Psi(x)
=

-\log V_A(x),
\]

where

\[
V_A(x)
\]

denotes the local admissible volume associated with the
neighborhood of (x).
\end{definition}

Large values of

\[
\Psi
\]

correspond to regions where admissible futures are scarce.

Small values correspond to regions possessing abundant
future flexibility.


Admissibility Curvature


Curvature measures how rapidly admissibility changes.

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Curvature Tensor}{adm-curvature-tensor}
The admissibility curvature tensor is

\[
K_{ij}
=

\nabla_i\nabla_j\Psi.
\]
\end{definition}

Equivalently,

\[
K_{ij}
=

*

\nabla_i\nabla_j
\log V_A.
\]

The tensor

\[
K_{ij}
\]

is the Hessian of the admissibility potential.

It measures second-order sensitivity of future
possibility.

\begin{definition}{Scalar Admissibility Curvature}{scalar-adm-curvature}
The scalar admissibility curvature is

\[
K_A
=

g^{ij}K_{ij}.
\]
\end{definition}


Interpretation of Curvature


The sign of admissibility curvature carries geometric
meaning.

\begin{prop}
Positive admissibility curvature corresponds to local
contraction of future possibility.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
If

\[
K_{ij}
=

\nabla_i\nabla_j\Psi
\]

is positive semidefinite, then

\[
\Psi
\]

is locally convex.

Since

\[
\Psi
=

-\log V_A,
\]

convexity of

\[
\Psi
\]

corresponds to local concavity of admissible volume.

Hence nearby perturbations reduce admissible volume.
\end{proof}

\begin{prop}
Negative admissibility curvature corresponds to local
expansion of future possibility.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
If

\[
K_{ij}
\]

is negative semidefinite then

\[
\Psi
\]

is locally concave.

Therefore

\[
V_A
\]

is locally expanding.

Small perturbations increase future possibility.
\end{proof}

Positive curvature therefore signals fragility.

Negative curvature signals generativity.


Boundary Geometry


The topology of admissibility is controlled by its
boundary.

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Boundary}{adm-boundary-app}
The admissibility boundary is

\[
\partial\mathcal A.
\]
\end{definition}

Points near

\[
\partial\mathcal A
\]

possess heightened sensitivity.

Small perturbations may move trajectories into
inadmissible regions.

\begin{definition}{Boundary Distance}{boundary-distance}
The boundary distance of state

\[
x
\]

is

\[
d_A(x)
=

d(x,\partial\mathcal A).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{prop}
Boundary distance provides a first-order measure of local
admissibility robustness.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For sufficiently small perturbations

\[
|\delta x|
<
d_A(x),
\]

the perturbed state remains inside

\[
\mathcal A.
\]

Hence larger boundary distances imply larger admissible
perturbation neighborhoods.
\end{proof}


The Admissibility Gradient


The gradient of admissibility volume determines the
direction of maximal expansion.

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Gradient}{adm-gradient}
The admissibility gradient is

\[
\nabla V_A.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{prop}
The direction

\[
\nabla V_A
\]

maximizes local increase in admissible volume.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For a differentiable scalar field the gradient points in
the direction of greatest increase.

Since

\[
V_A
\]

is a scalar field, the result follows directly from
multivariable calculus.
\end{proof}

The admissibility gradient therefore provides a natural
direction of generative motion.


Admissible Flows


Let

\[
\phi_t
\]

be a flow on the state space.

\begin{definition}{Admissible Flow}{adm-flow}
A flow is admissible if

\[
\phi_t(\mathcal A)
\subseteq
\mathcal A
\]

for all

\[
t\ge0.
\]
\end{definition}

Admissible flows preserve viability.

Many systems satisfy this condition only approximately.

Consequently admissibility must often be treated as a
stability problem.


Generative Admissibility


The central geometric principle of the framework is not
mere preservation but expansion.

\begin{definition}{Generative Admissibility}{generative-adm}
A system is generatively admissible if

\[
\frac{dV_A}{dt}
\ge
0.
\]
\end{definition}

This condition states that admissible volume must remain
constant or increase through time.

\begin{theorem}{Generative Admissibility Theorem}{generative-adm-theorem}
If

\[
\frac{dV_A}{dt}
\ge0,
\]

then admissibility entropy satisfies

\[
\frac{dS_A}{dt}
\ge0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since

\[
S_A
=

\log V_A,
\]

differentiation yields

\[
\frac{dS_A}{dt}
=

\frac1{V_A}
\frac{dV_A}{dt}.
\]

Because

\[
V_A>0
\]

and

\[
\frac{dV_A}{dt}\ge0,
\]

it follows that

\[
\frac{dS_A}{dt}
\ge0.
\]
\end{proof}

Thus generative admissibility corresponds to monotonic
growth of admissible future possibility.


The Collapse Condition


The opposite regime is equally important.

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Collapse}{adm-collapse}
Admissibility collapse occurs when

\[
V_A
\rightarrow0.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Collapse Theorem}{collapse-theorem}
If

\[
V_A(t)
\rightarrow0,
\]

then

\[
S_A(t)
\rightarrow-\infty.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since

\[
S_A
=

\log V_A,
\]

the result follows from

\[
\log x
\rightarrow
-\infty
\]

as

\[
x
\rightarrow
0^+.
\]
\end{proof}

Admissibility collapse therefore represents complete
destruction of future viable possibility.


Admissibility and Reachability


Reachability and admissibility are closely related but not
identical.

Reachability asks:

\[
\text{What futures can be reached?}
\]

Admissibility asks:

\[
\text{What futures should remain reachable?}
\]

Define admissible reachability volume

\[
V_{AR}
=

\mu(R_t\cap\mathcal A).
\]
