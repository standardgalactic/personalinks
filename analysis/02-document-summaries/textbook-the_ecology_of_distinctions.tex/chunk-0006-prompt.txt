\emph{Convergence.} Holling's distinction between engineering
resilience --- return time to a single equilibrium --- and
ecological resilience --- the amount of disturbance a system
can absorb before flipping into a qualitatively different
stability regime --- anticipates this chapter's distinction
between regeneration and mere recovery to a fixed reference
state \parencite{holling1973}. Both reject a single equilibrium
point as the right object to track.

\emph{Divergence.} Holling's resilience is fundamentally about
remaining within the \emph{same} basin of attraction: resilience
is measured by how much perturbation a basin can absorb before
the system shifts regime. This chapter's regeneration is not
about staying within a basin at all. A distinction ecology can
undergo arbitrarily large reorganisation --- the formal analogue
of Holling's regime shift --- and still count as fully
regenerative, provided repair capacity and diversity are
preserved or expanded through the transition
(\cref{thm:regen-ecology-thm}). This chapter's regeneration is
therefore compatible with, and often requires, exactly the kind
of basin-flipping change Holling's framework treats as the
outcome resilience is meant to resist.

\emph{Open question.} Whether there is a principled translation
of Holling's basin-and-threshold language into this chapter's
diversity and capacity language --- whether ``remaining within a
basin'' corresponds to some specific, identifiable condition on
$\kappa(\Sigma)$ --- or whether the two frameworks simply ask
different questions that happen to share vocabulary, is left
open.

From Entropy to Geometry

\label{sec:ch13-motivation}

The preceding chapters established a progression.
Distinctions are drawn at a cost. Information measures how
many distinctions a system supports. Entropy measures the
multiplicity hidden beneath distinction structures.
Histories carry information that states discard.
Recoverability determines whether dispersed distinctions
remain reconstructible. Repair restores distinctions when
recoverability permits. Regeneration preserves repair
capacity over time.

Each of these concepts concerns what a system \emph{is} or
\emph{has been}. The present chapter turns to a different
question: what can a system \emph{become}?

This is not merely a pragmatic question. The geometry of
future possibility turns out to be as determinate, as
measurable, and as theoretically consequential as entropy
or information. A system that has maintained all of its
distinctions perfectly, repaired every damage, and achieved
full regeneration may nevertheless face a contracted future
if earlier trajectories have closed off regions of
possibility space. Conversely, a system with incomplete
distinctions may preserve a rich geometry of futures simply
by avoiding the collapses that would have eliminated them.
The concept that formalises this intuition is
\emph{reachability}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction-ecology} showed that a single
distinction's prospects depend on the ecology of dependencies
surrounding it: the Ecological Fragility Theorem
(\cref{thm:eco-fragility}) characterised when local damage
cascades through a dependency network, and the Regenerative
Ecology Theorem (\cref{thm:regen-ecology-thm}) extended
regeneration from a single system to an entire population of
interacting distinctions. Both results describe what an
ecology currently maintains. Neither yet asks what an ecology
--- or a single system within one --- could still become if
its present trajectory were different. That is the question
this chapter takes up.


Why Control Theory's Reachability Is Not Enough

\label{sec:ch13-why-control-theory}

Reachability is not a new word in this book's vocabulary.
Control theory has used it for decades, in a precise and
operationally useful sense: given a dynamical system and an
admissible set of control inputs, the reachable set from
state $x$ is the collection of states the system can be
steered into within some time horizon
\parencite{kalman1960,sontag1998}. This is not a loose
precursor to the present chapter's concept; it is a genuine
special case, recovered when the state space $X$ is taken to
be an ordinary physical or engineering configuration space
and the dynamics $\Phi$ are taken to be governed by a
controllable input.

What control theory's reachable set does not, and was never
built to, address is reachability for distinction structures
rather than physical states. A control engineer asks which
configurations of a satellite, a chemical plant, or a robotic
arm can be reached from the present configuration. This
chapter asks a structurally identical question one level up:
which \emph{distinguishable futures} --- which configurations
of admissible distinctions, not merely physical coordinates
--- remain reachable from a system's present distinction
structure. The two questions coincide exactly when the
distinctions in play are already coextensive with the
physical state space, which is the classical
control-theoretic case. They diverge sharply once the state
space in question is a genome, an institution, or a
scientific discipline, where a great deal of what can or
cannot be reached is determined by distinction-level
structure --- recoverability, dependency, repair capacity ---
that has no representation in $X$ at all. Reachability volume,
as this chapter defines it, generalises the control-theoretic
notion by replacing a fixed physical state space with the
full distinction-ecological structure developed across
Parts~I through~IV.


Common Misinterpretations of Reachability

\label{sec:ch13-misinterpretations}

A first misreading treats reachability volume as simply a
restatement of entropy --- more reachable states sounding
like more multiplicity, in the sense of
Chapter~\ref{ch:entropy}. The two are related but distinct.
Entropy measures multiplicity concealed beneath a single
distinction at a single moment; reachability volume measures
the size of the set of \emph{future} distinction structures
attainable from the present one. A system can have low
present entropy --- a sharply resolved, highly distinguished
current state --- while still possessing enormous
reachability volume, if that sharp state happens to sit at a
branch point from which many futures remain open.

A second misreading assumes that reachability volume can only
shrink, since entropy left unrepaired tends to grow and
distinctions left undefended tend to erode. The Reachability
Monotonicity Theorem proved below shows the relationship is
more specific than this intuition suggests: reachability
volume is non-increasing only under particular dynamical
conditions, and the same regenerative processes established
in Chapter~\ref{ch:regeneration} can expand it. Reachability
contraction is common, not inevitable.

A third misreading treats reachability as purely a matter of
quantity --- bigger reachable sets are simply better, full
stop. This is the question Chapter~\ref{ch:admissibility}
exists to complicate. Two states can have identical
reachability volume while differing enormously in which
specific futures that volume contains, a distinction
invisible to volume alone and the entire subject of the next
chapter.

By the end of this chapter, reachability sets and reachability
volume will have been defined formally as properties of a
dynamical system's distinguishable futures, the Reachability
Monotonicity Theorem will characterise when volume can only
shrink, and the Future Cone Theorem will describe the
geometry of the boundary separating reachable from
unreachable futures. Chapter~\ref{ch:admissibility} then asks
the question this chapter's volume measure cannot answer on
its own: among the states a system can reach, which ones are
worth reaching --- in the specific sense of preserving, rather
than foreclosing, further reachability?


Basic Definitions

\label{sec:ch13-defs}

\begin{definition}{State Space and Dynamics}{state-space}
  Let $X$ be a \emph{state space}: a measurable space whose
  points represent possible configurations of a system. A
  \emph{dynamics} on $X$ is a family of maps
  $\Phi_{s,t} : X \to X$, $s \le t$, satisfying
  $\Phi_{t,t} = \mathrm{id}$ and
  $\Phi_{s,t} = \Phi_{u,t} \circ \Phi_{s,u}$ for all
  $s \le u \le t$. The pair $(X,\Phi)$ is a \emph{dynamical
  system}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Reachability Set and Volume}{reach-set}
  Let $(X,\Phi)$ be a dynamical system, $x\in X$ a state,
  and $t_0 \le t$ times. The \emph{reachability set} of $x$
  at time $t$ is
  \[
    \reach(x,t_0,t) = \{
      y\in X : \exists\, \text{admissible }
      \gamma:[t_0,t]\to X,\;
      \gamma(t_0)=x,\; \gamma(t)=y
    \}.
  \]
  The \emph{reachability volume} is
  $V_R(x,t) = \mu(\reach(x,t))$ for reference measure $\mu$.
  \index{reachability!volume|textbf}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}{What counts as admissible?}{reach-admissible}
  The qualifier ``admissible trajectory'' is intentionally
  broad at this stage. In physical systems it may mean
  trajectories satisfying the equations of motion. In
  biological systems it may mean developmental programmes
  consistent with genetic and environmental constraints. In
  computational systems it may mean execution paths
  consistent with a program's operational semantics.
  Chapter~\ref{ch:admissibility} imposes the stronger
  condition of \emph{admissibility} in the technical sense,
  which adds a future-preservation requirement on top of
  bare reachability.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}{Reachability in a Finite Automaton}{reach-dfa}
  \csicon
  Let $X = \{q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3\}$ be the states of a
  deterministic finite automaton with transition function
  $\delta$. Define reachability at depth $n$ as the set of
  states accessible from $q_0$ via paths of length at most
  $n$. With counting measure, $V_R(q_0,n) = |\reach(q_0,n)|$.
  If $\delta$ is a bijection, $V_R$ is constant. If some
  states are absorbing (no outgoing transitions), $V_R$
  declines as the automaton is driven into them.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{Reachability in a Fitness Landscape}{reach-fitness}
  \bioicon
  In evolutionary biology, a \emph{fitness landscape} maps
  genotypes $g\in X$ to fitness values $f(g)\in\mathbb{R}$.
  The reachability set of a population at genotype $g$
  under point-mutation dynamics is the set of genotypes
  accessible by single mutations without crossing a fitness
  valley of depth exceeding some threshold $\theta$.
  Reachability volume measures the accessible adaptive
  neighbourhood.
\end{example}

\begin{distinctionaside}{The Empty Refrigerator}
An empty refrigerator and a full refrigerator may contain
the same number of sandwiches right now: zero.

The difference is reachability. One state is adjacent to
lunch. The other is adjacent to grocery shopping.
\end{distinctionaside}


Reachability Monotonicity

\label{sec:ch13-monotone}

\begin{theorem}{Reachability Monotonicity Theorem}{reach-mono}
  Let $(X,\Phi)$ be a dynamical system with
  entropy-increasing dynamics, and let
  $\mathcal{C}_1\subseteq\mathcal{C}_2$ be two constraint
  sets imposed on trajectories from $x$. Then
  \[
    V_R(x,t\mid\mathcal{C}_2) \le V_R(x,t\mid\mathcal{C}_1).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Denote by $\reach(x,t\mid\mathcal{C})$ the set of states
  reachable from $x$ by time $t$ via trajectories satisfying
  constraint set $\mathcal{C}$. Since
  $\mathcal{C}_1\subseteq\mathcal{C}_2$, every trajectory
  satisfying $\mathcal{C}_2$ also satisfies $\mathcal{C}_1$.
  Therefore
  $\reach(x,t\mid\mathcal{C}_2) \subseteq \reach(x,t\mid\mathcal{C}_1)$,
  and applying the measure $\mu$ to both sides gives
  $V_R(x,t\mid\mathcal{C}_2) \le V_R(x,t\mid\mathcal{C}_1)$.
  \parencite{sontag1998}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}{Constraint Accumulation}{constraint-accum}
  If a system accumulates constraints over time,
  $\mathcal{C}(t_1)\subseteq\mathcal{C}(t_2)$ for
  $t_1\le t_2$, then
  $V_R(x,T\mid\mathcal{C}(t_2)) \le V_R(x,T\mid\mathcal{C}(t_1))$
  for any future time $T\ge t_2$.
\end{corollary}

The corollary captures a central ecological intuition:
systems that accumulate constraints without shedding them
eventually approach reachability exhaustion. Part~IX
applies this directly to fiscal and governance systems.


The Constraint Volume Theorem

\label{sec:ch13-constraint-vol}

The Reachability Monotonicity Theorem is qualitative. The
following theorem makes the relationship between constraint
density and reachability loss quantitative.

\begin{definition}{Constraint Density}{constraint-density}
  Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a set of trajectory constraints, each
  eliminating a measurable subset of $X$. The
  \emph{constraint density} at $x$ is
  \[
    \rho_\mathcal{C}(x)
    = \frac{1}{\mu(X)} \sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
      \mu(\{y : c\text{ eliminates }y\}).
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Constraint Volume Theorem}{constraint-vol}
  Under independence assumptions on constraint impacts,
  reachability volume satisfies
  \[
    V_R(x,t) \le \mu(X)\, e^{-\rho_\mathcal{C}|\mathcal{C}|}.
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Under independence, each constraint eliminates fraction
  $\rho_\mathcal{C}$ of the remaining reachable set
  independently. After $|\mathcal{C}|$ constraints, the
  surviving fraction is
  $(1-\rho_\mathcal{C})^{|\mathcal{C}|}$. Using the
  inequality $1-p\le e^{-p}$ for $p\in[0,1]$,
  \[
    V_R(x,t)
    \le \mu(X)(1-\rho_\mathcal{C})^{|\mathcal{C}|}
    \le \mu(X)\,e^{-\rho_\mathcal{C}|\mathcal{C}|}. \qedhere
  \]
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Exponential Collapse}{exp-collapse}
  The Constraint Volume Theorem reveals that reachability
  volume decays \emph{at least exponentially} in constraint
  count under independence. This is structurally analogous
  to the exponential decay of partition functions under
  constraint in statistical mechanics, and to the
  exponential narrowing of hypothesis spaces under
  independent observations in Bayesian inference.
  Dependence among constraints can accelerate or decelerate
  this decay, but cannot eliminate it if constraints
  genuinely eliminate distinct regions of future space.
\end{remark}


Reachability Boundaries

\label{sec:ch13-boundary}

\begin{definition}{Reachability Boundary}{reach-boundary}
  The \emph{reachability boundary} of $x$ at time $t$ is
  \[
    \partial\reach(x,t)
    = \overline{\reach(x,t)} \setminus
      \mathrm{int}(\reach(x,t)),
  \]
  where $\mathrm{int}(\cdot)$ denotes interior in the
  topology of $X$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Boundary Proximity}{boundary-prox}
  The \emph{boundary proximity} of a state
  $y\in\reach(x,t)$ is
  $\beta(y,x,t) = d(y,\partial\reach(x,t))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Boundary Proximity and Critical Instability}
  {boundary-instab}
  Let $(X,\Phi)$ be a smooth dynamical system. As
  $\beta(y,x,t)\to0$, the sensitivity of future
  reachability volume to perturbations diverges:
  \[
    \left\|\frac{\partial V_R}{\partial\epsilon}\right\|
    \to \infty \quad \text{as } \beta \to 0.
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Consider a trajectory $\gamma$ terminating near
  $\partial\reach(x,t)$. A perturbation $\epsilon$ to
  $\gamma$ displaces the terminal state by order $\epsilon$.
  Near the boundary, a displacement of size $\epsilon$ can
  either remain inside $\reach(x,t)$ or exit it, eliminating
  an entire connected component of future reachability. The
  change in $V_R$ is therefore not $O(\epsilon)$ but $O(1)$
  in the worst case as $\beta\to0$. Hence
  $\|\partial V_R/\partial\epsilon\|\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Ecological Interpretation}{boundary-eco}
  Boundary proximity is a precise analogue of ecological
  \emph{edge effects}. A species population near the
  boundary of its viable climate envelope is
  disproportionately sensitive to small perturbations. An
  institution near the boundary of its fiscal reachability
  set is disproportionately sensitive to minor shocks. A
  computation near the boundary of its termination condition
  is disproportionately sensitive to numerical error. In
  each case, the geometry of the reachability boundary ---
  not the current state alone --- determines fragility.
\end{remark}


The Future Cone Theorem

\label{sec:ch13-future-cone}

Under broad conditions, reachability sets inherit a
cone-like structure from the underlying dynamics. This is
the geometric heart of the chapter.

\begin{definition}{Future Cone}{future-cone-def}
  The \emph{future cone} of $x$ from time $t_0$ is the union
  over all future times:
  \[
    \mathcal{F}(x,t_0) = \bigcup_{t\ge t_0} \reach(x,t_0,t)
    \subseteq X \times [t_0,\infty).
  \]
  \index{future cone|textbf}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Future Cone Theorem}{future-cone}
  For any dynamical system $(X,\Phi)$ and initial state $x$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item $\mathcal{F}(x,t_0)$ is non-empty for all $t_0$.
    \item For $s\le t$, $\reach(x,t_0,s) \subseteq
          \reach(x,t_0,t)$ whenever the dynamics are
          non-contracting.
    \item The boundary $\partial\mathcal{F}(x,t_0)$ is a
          codimension-one surface in
          $X\times[t_0,\infty)$ whenever $X$ is a smooth
          manifold and $\Phi$ is smooth.
    \item Collapse of $\mathcal{F}(x,t_0)$ to a
          lower-dimensional set is equivalent to the system
          becoming trapped in a proper invariant
          submanifold.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  (i) The trivial trajectory $\gamma(t)=x$ for all $t$ is
  always admissible, so $x\in\reach(x,t_0,t)$ for all $t$.

  (ii) Under non-contracting dynamics, any state reachable
  at time $s$ remains reachable or leads to further states
  at time $t>s$. Hence
  $\reach(x,t_0,s)\subseteq\reach(x,t_0,t)$.

  (iii) By the implicit function theorem, the boundary of
  $\mathcal{F}(x,t_0)$ is locally a smooth hypersurface
  wherever $\Phi$ is smooth and transversality conditions
  hold.

  (iv) If $\mathcal{F}(x,t_0)\subseteq M \subsetneq X$ for a
  proper submanifold $M$, then $M$ is forward-invariant
  under $\Phi$ restricted to trajectories from $x$.
  Conversely, if the system is trapped in such an $M$, the
  reachability set collapses to at most $\dim M < \dim X$
  dimensions.
\end{proof}

Reachability and Entropy

\label{sec:ch13-entropy}

Chapter~\ref{ch:entropy} established that entropy measures
latent multiplicity beneath a distinction structure. We can
now close the loop: entropy and reachability volume are
related by a precise inequality.

\begin{theorem}{Entropy--Reachability Inequality}{entropy-reach}
  For a system with state space $X$ equipped with a
  uniform measure $\mu$,
  \[
    S(x,t) \le k\log V_R(x,t) + S_0,
  \]
  where $k$ is the Boltzmann constant (or its informational
  analogue $\log_2 e$ in nats/bits).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Entropy $S(x,t)=k\log\Omega(x,t)$ where $\Omega$ counts
  microstates compatible with the current macrostate. Every
  compatible microstate is reachable from some earlier
  state, so $\Omega(x,t)\le\mu(\reach(x,t))=V_R(x,t)$ up to
  normalisation. The inequality follows from monotonicity
  of $\log$.
\end{proof}

The inequality is in general not tight: reachability volume
upper-bounds entropy because the reachability set includes
states that may be accessible but not thermodynamically
populated. Chapter~\ref{ch:admissibility} tightens this to
the admissible subset.


Reachability in Computation

\label{sec:ch13-computation}

\csicon
Reachability is a classical concept in theoretical computer
science, where it underlies model checking, program
verification, and complexity theory. The
distinction-theoretic framing adds a quantitative geometric
layer that classical reachability analysis typically omits.

\begin{definition}{Computational Reachability Volume}{comp-reach}
  Let $M$ be a Turing machine with configuration space $C$.
  The \emph{computational reachability volume} from
  configuration $c_0$ after $t$ steps is
  $V_R^M(c_0,t) = |\reach_M(c_0,t)|$, the number of
  distinct configurations reachable within $t$ computation
  steps.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}{Halting and Reachability Collapse}{halt-reach}
  A Turing machine $M$ halts on input $w$ if and only if
  the reachability set eventually collapses to a singleton:
  \[
    \exists\, t^* :
    \reach_M(c_0(w),t) = \{c_\mathrm{halt}\}
    \quad \forall\, t \ge t^*.
  \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  If $M$ halts at time $t^*$, the halting configuration
  $c_\mathrm{halt}$ has no outgoing transitions. Hence for
  all $t\ge t^*$, the only reachable configuration is
  $c_\mathrm{halt}$. Conversely, if reachability collapses
  to a singleton, that singleton must be a configuration
  with no transitions, i.e.\ a halting configuration.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Undecidability as Reachability Uncertainty}{undecide-reach}
  The undecidability of the halting problem is, in
  reachability terms, the statement that one cannot
  determine in advance whether $V_R^M(c_0,t)$ will collapse
  to a singleton. This is directly analogous to the
  statement that one cannot determine from a present state
  alone whether a trajectory approaches a boundary of the
  reachability set.
\end{remark}


Reachability in Biology

\label{sec:ch13-biology}

\bioicon
Biological systems operate under reachability constraints
at every scale.

\paragraph{Development.} A totipotent stem cell has maximal
developmental reachability: it can become any cell type.
Differentiation is irreversible commitment to a proper
subset of fates. Waddington's epigenetic landscape is a
qualitative picture of the reachability structure of
developmental trajectories \parencite{waddington1957strategy}.
Reachability volume decreases monotonically during normal
development (\cref{thm:reach-mono}); reprogramming
(e.g.\ induced pluripotency) partially restores it.

\paragraph{Immune memory.} An immune system that has
encountered antigen $a$ has a different reachability set
than a naive immune system. Vaccination expands
reachability volume with respect to pathogen response while
leaving most of state space unchanged. Immunosuppression
contracts it.

\paragraph{Ecology.} A food web defines reachability
constraints among species: which trophic configurations are
accessible from a given community composition. Extinction
collapses reachability by permanently removing nodes from
the network. The Constraint Volume Theorem
(\cref{thm:constraint-vol}) predicts exponential narrowing
of ecological reachability under sequential extinction.


Reachability and the Distinction Framework

\label{sec:ch13-connection}

We close the chapter by connecting reachability geometry
back to the distinction-theoretic foundations of Part~I.

\begin{theorem}{Reachability as Distinction Preservation}
  {reach-dist}
  A trajectory $\gamma:[t_0,t]\to X$ is
  reachability-preserving if and only if it does not reduce
  the number of distinctions that can be produced from any
  state along $\gamma$. Formally,
  \[
    V_R(\gamma(t),t) \ge V_R(\gamma(t_0),t_0)
    \iff
    D(\gamma(t)) \ge D(\gamma(t_0)).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Each reachable state $y\in\reach(x,t)$ corresponds to a
  distinct future distinction structure. A reduction in
  $V_R$ therefore corresponds to the elimination of entire
  families of distinction structures from the future.
  Conversely, preservation of $V_R$ preserves the range of
  distinction structures accessible from $\gamma(t)$. The
  equivalence follows from the bijection between reachable
  states and achievable distinction structures under the
  assumption that distinct states support distinct
  distinctions.
\end{proof}

This theorem is the bridge to Chapter~\ref{ch:admissibility}.
Reachability asks which states are accessible.
Admissibility asks which of those states preserves the
capacity to reach further states --- and, ultimately, to
produce further distinctions.


Related Frameworks

\label{sec:ch13-related-frameworks}

\subsection*{Information Geometry}

\emph{Convergence.} Information geometry equips spaces of
probability distributions with a canonical Riemannian
structure --- the Fisher information metric --- turning
families of distributions into manifolds on which distances,
geodesics, and volumes are well defined
\parencite{amari2016,ay2017}. This chapter's Reachability Set
and Volume (\cref{defn:reach-set}) treats reachability in
exactly the same spirit: not as a mere list of accessible
states but as a measurable volume, $V_R$, in a genuinely
geometric space.

\emph{Divergence.} Information geometry's metric is induced by
the curvature of a statistical model itself --- how
distinguishable nearby distributions are under repeated
sampling --- a property specifically of probability measures.
This chapter's reachability volume is induced by dynamics, not
statistics: $V_R(x,t)$ is the volume of states accessible from
$x$ under whatever flow or transition structure governs the
system, well defined even for purely deterministic systems with
no probabilistic interpretation at all. The Reachability
Monotonicity Theorem (\cref{thm:reach-mono}) is a dynamical, not
a statistical, fact.

\emph{Open question.} Whether a Fisher-Rao-style metric could be
constructed directly on the reachable sets themselves --
treating each $\reach(x,t)$ as an empirical distribution over
future states -- yielding a genuinely information-geometric
reading of reachability volume, is not pursued here.

\subsection*{Dynamical Systems Theory}
\index{reachability!control-theory contrast|textbf}

\emph{Convergence.} The classical treatment of basins of
attraction, invariant sets, and forward-orbit closures
\parencite{strogatz2015,smale1967} is the most direct
mathematical antecedent of this chapter's reachable set
$\reach(x,t)$: the forward orbit of $x$ under a system's flow,
or its closure, is exactly the structure this chapter measures.

\emph{Divergence.} Classical dynamical systems theory is
centrally interested in long-run, asymptotic orbit behaviour ---
attractors, limit cycles, basins --- largely independent of any
value attached to where an orbit ends up. This chapter's
apparatus instead treats the \emph{size} of the reachable set
at every finite time $t$ as the primary object, via the
Reachability Monotonicity Theorem and the Constraint Volume
Theorem (\cref{thm:constraint-vol}), because the chapter's
downstream concern --- developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:admissibility}
--- is precisely which of these reachable futures preserve
value, not merely where the system eventually settles.

\emph{Open question.} Whether $V_R$'s monotonicity properties
correspond cleanly to Liouville-theorem-style volume
preservation for Hamiltonian flows \parencite{arnold1989}, or
whether dissipative systems, where phase-space volume
contracts, require a separate treatment within this chapter's
framework, is left open.

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\begin{chapsummary}
  \item Reachability sets formalise what a system can
        become.
  \item $V_R$ is the primary quantitative measure of future
        possibility.
  \item Stronger constraints monotonically reduce $V_R$
        (\cref{thm:reach-mono}).
  \item Constraint accumulation causes at least exponential
        decay of reachability (\cref{thm:constraint-vol}).
  \item States near the reachability boundary exhibit
        critical instability (\cref{thm:boundary-instab}).
  \item Under smooth, non-contracting dynamics, reachable
        futures form a cone-like structure
        (\cref{thm:future-cone}).
  \item Entropy is bounded above by the log of reachability
        volume (\cref{thm:entropy-reach}).
  \item In computation, halting equals reachability
        collapse; in biology, differentiation, immunity, and
        extinction are all reachability-reducing events.
  \item Reachability preservation is equivalent to
        distinction capacity preservation
        (\cref{thm:reach-dist}).
\end{chapsummary}

The Insufficiency of Reachability

\label{sec:ch14-insufficiency}

Chapter~\ref{ch:reachability} established that reachability
volume is the primary quantitative measure of a system's
future possibility. But reachability alone is insufficient
as a criterion for evaluating trajectories. Consider two
trajectories $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ from the same initial
state $x$, both terminating at time $T$ with identical
reachability volumes,
$V_R(\gamma_1(T),T) = V_R(\gamma_2(T),T)$. Are they
equivalent? Not necessarily. It matters \emph{which} states
are reachable, not merely \emph{how many}.

\begin{example}{Two Trajectories with Equal Volume}{equal-vol}
  \bioicon
  Consider an immune system that has fought off infection
  via two strategies. Strategy $\gamma_1$ produces a diverse
  antibody repertoire, reaching many distinct antigenic
  configurations. Strategy $\gamma_2$ produces a narrow,
  highly optimised antibody response, reaching an equal
  \emph{number} of configurations but concentrated in a
  single antigenic region. Both strategies give
  $V_R(\gamma_i(T),T)=k$ for the same $k$. But $\gamma_2$
  has exhausted cross-reactive potential: a novel antigen
  outside its narrow repertoire will find the immune system
  unable to respond. $\gamma_1$ preserves the capacity to
  respond to novel threats. Reachability volume is the
  same. Admissibility is not.
\end{example}

\begin{distinctionaside}{The Meeting That Could Have Been an
  Email}
A one-hour meeting removes one hour from ten people. The
meeting therefore destroys ten hours of future possibility.

To justify itself, it must generate more future possibility
than it consumes. Most meetings fail this test.
\end{distinctionaside}

\begin{envelopeaside}{The Cost of Meetings}
Ten people spend one hour in a meeting. Current cost:
$10$ person-hours.

Future cost: every delayed decision changes future reachable
states. The true cost is not the hour consumed. It is the
reachable volume destroyed by consuming it.

Most calendars ignore the larger term.
\end{envelopeaside}

This motivates the central concept of the chapter.
\emph{Admissibility} refines reachability by asking not
merely whether a state is reachable, but whether reaching
it preserves the capacity for further reachability ---
including the reachability of further admissible states.


Why Feasibility Is Not the Same as Admissibility

\label{sec:ch14-why-feasibility}

Many disciplines already possess concepts that resemble
admissibility on the surface. Engineers speak of feasible
designs: a design is feasible if it satisfies every specified
constraint --- structural, thermal, budgetary --- at the
moment of evaluation. Optimisation theory speaks of a
feasible region: the subset of a search space satisfying a
problem's constraints, within which an objective function is
then optimised. Constraint satisfaction in this sense asks a
single, static question: does a candidate solution violate
any constraint, right now?

Admissibility asks a different and more demanding question. A
state can be perfectly feasible --- satisfying every
constraint imposed on it --- while the act of reaching that
state forecloses the future feasibility of states that would
otherwise have remained available. Feasibility, as ordinarily
used, is evaluated at a single instant against a fixed
constraint set. Admissibility is evaluated against a
trajectory's effect on the constraint set itself, asking not
merely whether the present state is acceptable but whether
reaching it leaves the system's future similarly well
supplied with acceptable states. A feasible trajectory can be
admissibility-destroying: the immune system's narrow,
highly-optimised antibody response above is feasible by every
ordinary criterion --- it satisfies the constraint of fighting
off the present infection --- and admissibility-destroying
all the same. Feasibility is a property of a single state
relative to a fixed constraint set; admissibility is a
property of a trajectory relative to the constraint sets its
own continuation will face.


Common Misinterpretations of Admissibility

\label{sec:ch14-misinterpretations}

A first misreading treats admissibility as simply a stricter
or more conservative feasibility criterion --- as though
admissible states were just a smaller, safer subset of
feasible ones. This is not the relationship. A state can be
admissible while being only narrowly feasible by ordinary
standards, if reaching it preserves substantial future
reachability despite operating close to some constraint
boundary; and a state can be comfortably feasible while being
inadmissible, as the chapter's opening example shows.
Admissibility and feasibility are evaluated along different
axes entirely, not nested subsets of the same axis.

A second misreading equates admissibility with utility or
preference satisfaction --- that an admissible trajectory is
simply one a rational agent would prefer. The Admissibility
Manifold defined below is constructed without reference to
any agent's preferences at all; it depends only on the
structure of future reachability, the same structure
Chapter~\ref{ch:reachability} formalised independently of what
anyone wants. An agent may prefer an inadmissible trajectory
for reasons entirely orthogonal to admissibility, and nothing
in this chapter's machinery forbids that; what the chapter
does establish, and what later chapters build upon, is that
such a preference comes at a specific, measurable cost in
future reachable volume.

A third misreading equates admissibility with mere survival
--- that as long as a system continues to exist, it remains
admissible by default. Chapter~\ref{ch:beyond-continuation}
already showed that continuation alone cannot distinguish
healthy persistence from pathological persistence; the same
gap reappears here. A system can survive indefinitely along a
trajectory that steadily narrows its admissible future, right
up to a final state from which almost nothing further is
reachable. Survival says a system is still there. Admissibility
says something about what it can still become.

By the end of this chapter, the admissibility manifold
$\adm(x,t)$ will have been defined formally, the Admissibility
Existence Theorem will guarantee its existence under stated
conditions, and the Projection--Admissibility Gap Theorem will
show precisely how much admissibility-relevant structure a
naive feasibility check discards. Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry-futures}
then extends this chapter's manifold into a fuller invariant
--- adding curvature and topology to the volume measure
developed here --- and asks whether the resulting
preservation criterion can be derived as a strategic
conclusion rather than assumed as a value judgement.


The Admissibility Manifold

\label{sec:ch14-manifold}

\begin{definition}{Future Reachability Function}{future-reach}
  For a state $y\in X$ and time $t$, define the
  \emph{future reachability function}
  $F(y,t,\tau) = V_R(y,t,t+\tau)$: the reachability volume
  available from $y$ over a further time interval of length
  $\tau > 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Threshold}{adm-threshold}
  Fix a threshold $\alpha>0$ and a horizon $\tau>0$. A
  state $y\in X$ is \emph{$(\alpha,\tau)$-admissible} from
  $x$ at time $t$ if $y\in\reach(x,t_0,t)$ and
  $F(y,t,\tau) \ge \alpha\cdot V_R(x,t_0,t)$: the future
  reachable from $y$ retains at least fraction $\alpha$ of
  the reachability volume that was available at the start.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Manifold}{adm-manifold}
  The \emph{admissibility manifold} is the closure of the
  set of $(\alpha,\tau)$-admissible states:
  \[
    \adm(x,t_0,t)
    = \overline{\{
        y \in \reach(x,t_0,t) :
        F(y,t,\tau) \ge \alpha\cdot V_R(x,t_0,t)
      \}}.
  \]
  \index{admissibility!manifold|textbf}
\end{definition}

The admissibility manifold is a subset of the reachability
set: $\adm(t) \subseteq \reach(x,t_0,t)$. It contains those
reachable states from which the future
remains open.\index{future cone!relation to A(x,t)|textbf}

Admissibility Existence

\label{sec:ch14-existence}

\begin{theorem}{Admissibility Existence Theorem}{adm-exist}
  Let $(X,\Phi)$ be a compact dynamical system with
  continuous $F(\cdot,t,\tau)$ for all $t,\tau>0$. Then for
  any $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and $\tau>0$, the admissibility
  manifold $\adm(x,t)$ is non-empty whenever $\reach(x,t)$
  is non-empty and $V_R(x,t)>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Since $F(y,t,\tau)$ is continuous on the compact set
  $\reach(x,t)$, it attains its maximum at some
  $y^*\in\reach(x,t)$. Let $V^* = F(y^*,t,\tau)$. We need
  $V^* \ge \alpha\cdot V_R(x,t_0,t)$. At minimum, the
  trivial continuation $\gamma(s)=y^*$ for
  $s\in[t,t+\tau]$ gives $F(y^*,t,\tau)\ge0$. Since
  $\reach(x,t)$ is non-empty and compact, and $F$ is
  continuous, the supremum is attained. If $V_R(x,t)>0$
  then $\reach(x,t)$ contains states with non-trivial
  forward dynamics, so $V^*>0$. Choosing
  $\alpha < V^*/V_R(x,t_0,t)$ guarantees
  $\adm(x,t)\ne\emptyset$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Non-triviality of the threshold}{adm-threshold-rmk}
  The Admissibility Existence Theorem guarantees that
  $\adm$ is non-empty but does not guarantee it contains
  trajectories that are practically useful. For large
  $\alpha$ (requiring the future to be nearly as open as
  the present), the admissibility manifold may be very
  small. The choice of $\alpha$ is a design parameter
  reflecting how strongly a system values future openness
  versus present gain. Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry-futures}
  discusses how this parameter connects to generative
  versus extractive trajectories.
\end{remark}
