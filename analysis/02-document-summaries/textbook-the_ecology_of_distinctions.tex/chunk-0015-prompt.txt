The Wider Stakes: Distinction Diversity at National Scale

field-level terminology for what this chapter's formalism
describes as $\Gamma_\mathcal{I} < 0$ sustained long enough to
hollow an institution from the inside while its formal shell
persists. Condition (i) of the Democratic Reachability Theorem,
distinction diversity among independent institutional systems, is
not violated by any single newspaper's decline; it is violated by
this pattern repeating at scale, across thousands of communities,
simultaneously.

Science as Ecology


Kuhn's account of scientific revolutions
\parencite{kuhn1962structure} identified the structure
of normal science and paradigm shifts but did not
provide a quantitative theory distinguishing healthy
science from pathological science. The distinction-
theoretic framework does.

Popper's falsificationism comes at the same problem from
the opposite direction \parencite{popper1959}: a theory
counts as scientific only if it forbids some observable
outcome, and progress consists in subjecting theories to
attempts at refutation. This sharpens Kuhn's descriptive
picture into a normative criterion, but it is still a
binary test applied theory by theory, not a measure of
how much capacity a scientific community retains to
generate and resolve anomalies over time. A field can
satisfy Popper's falsifiability criterion at the level of
individual hypotheses while still losing, gradually and
institutionally, the methodological diversity and training
pipeline that this chapter's Regenerative Epistemology
Theorem identifies as necessary for long-run health.

Chapter~\ref{ch:governance} closed by noting that science's
capacity to revise its own foundational commitments is among
the clearest real-world instances of the meta-repair
condition identified there as the hardest for any institution
to satisfy. This chapter takes up that case directly: science
is the institution explicitly built around making meta-repair
--- revision of its own methods of revision --- a permanent
and celebrated feature rather than a rare crisis response.

Science is a distinction ecology in the sense of
Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction-ecology}. The distinctions
are theoretical categories: the partitions that
separate explananda from noise, signal from artifact,
valid inference from fallacy, confirmed hypothesis
from speculation. The dependency relation
$\mathcal{L}_S$ encodes which theoretical distinctions
presuppose which others. The repair algebra
$\mathcal{R}_S$ includes peer review, replication,
meta-analysis, theoretical revision, and paradigm shift.

What distinguishes science from other distinction
ecologies is the \emph{anomaly commitment}: science
explicitly preserves anomalies rather than suppressing
them. A healthy scientific community maintains a
non-empty failure manifold $\mathcal{F}_S$ and
actively invests in its resolution.

\begin{definition}{Scientific Distinction Ecology}{sci-ecology}
  \index{ecology!scientific communities as|textbf}
  A \emph{scientific distinction ecology} is a tuple
  $\mathcal{S} = (\mathcal{D}_S, \mathcal{L}_S,
  \mathcal{R}_S, \mathcal{F}_S, \mathcal{Q}_S)$ where:
  \begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathcal{D}_S$: the set of active theoretical
          distinctions.
    \item $\mathcal{L}_S$: the dependency relation
          (which theories presuppose which others).
    \item $\mathcal{R}_S$: the repair algebra
          (experimental, statistical, and theoretical
          revision mechanisms).
    \item $\mathcal{F}_S = \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D}_S,
          \mathcal{R}_S)$: the current failure manifold
          (open anomalies).
    \item $\mathcal{Q}_S$: the \emph{question space} —
          the set of distinctions science can currently
          pose but not yet resolve.
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Scientific Reachability Volume}{sci-reach}
  The \emph{scientific reachability volume} is
  \[
    V_S(t)
    = \mu_S\!\bigl(\mathcal{Q}_S(t) \cup \adm_S(t)\bigr),
  \]
  the measure of questions science can currently pose
  (the question space) plus its admissible future
  (the currently approachable theoretical territory).
\end{definition}


The Scientific Repair Theorem


\begin{theorem}{Scientific Repair Theorem}{sci-repair}
  Science performs admissible repair iff each revision
  of $\mathcal{D}_S$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item Resolves at least one anomaly in $\mathcal{F}_S$.
    \item Preserves all distinctions in $\mathcal{D}_S$
          not implicated in the resolved anomaly.
    \item Does not introduce new anomalies faster than
          it resolves existing ones.
  \end{enumerate}
  A revision satisfying (i)--(iii) is
  \emph{scientifically admissible}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Ontology Revision Theorem
  (\cref{thm:ont-revision}), an admissible ontological
  enlargement resolves anomalies while preserving
  existing distinctions (condition~(i) of
  Definition~\ref{defn:ont-enlarge}).
  (i) corresponds to condition~(iii) of the definition:
  persistent anomalies are resolved.
  (ii) corresponds to condition~(i): the embedding is
  isometric.
  (iii) is the additional scientific requirement:
  new distinctions introduced by the revision should
  not generate new failure manifold faster than they
  shrink the existing one, which would indicate a
  net reduction in $V_S$ and hence violation of
  admissibility (\cref{prop:repair-adm}).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}{General Relativity as Admissible Repair}
  {gr-adm-repair}
  General relativity satisfies (i)--(iii).
  (i) It resolves the persistent anomaly of Mercury's
  perihelion and the Michelson-Morley result.
  (ii) It preserves Newtonian mechanics as a limiting
  case (the equivalence principle ensures the
  Newtonian prediction is recovered for
  $v \ll c$, $GM/r \ll c^2$).
  (iii) The new distinctions it introduces — spacetime
  curvature, gravitational waves, frame dragging —
  generate a vastly expanded question space $\mathcal{Q}_S$
  whose anomalies are actively productive rather than
  stagnating.
\end{example}


The Question Generation Theorem


The most important property of healthy science is not
that it resolves anomalies but that it generates more
questions than it closes. This is the scientific
analogue of generative admissibility.

\begin{theorem}{Question Generation Theorem}{question-gen}
  A scientific tradition is generatively admissible iff
  \[
    \frac{d}{dt}|\mathcal{Q}_S(t)| \;\ge\; 0:
  \]
  the question space does not contract.
  Furthermore, a \emph{great theory} is one for which
  \[
    \frac{d}{dt}|\mathcal{Q}_S(t)| \gg 0:
  \]
  it expands the question space strictly and rapidly.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Generative Admissibility Principle
  (\cref{prc:gap-ch15}), a system is generatively
  admissible iff it preserves the capacity for future
  distinction-production.
  In science, future distinction-production corresponds
  to future question resolution.
  The capacity for future question resolution is bounded
  by the current question space: questions not yet
  posed cannot yet be resolved.
  Therefore preserving distinction-production capacity
  requires preserving or expanding $\mathcal{Q}_S$.
  $|\mathcal{Q}_S(t)|$ non-decreasing is the
  scientific instantiation of
  $\frac{d}{dt}\Vol(\adm(t)) \ge 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{The failure of paradigm-ending theories}{paradigm-end}
  The Question Generation Theorem explains why some
  apparently successful theories are scientifically
  problematic. A theory that claims to explain
  everything within its domain while generating no
  new questions is extractive: it resolves existing
  anomalies by eliminating the distinctions that
  would generate new ones.

  A theory of everything that provides exactly one
  prediction has $|\mathcal{Q}_S| = 1$ after
  its adoption: it has collapsed the question space
  to a single empirical test. If the test fails,
  the entire question space collapses to zero.
  If it succeeds, science loses the productive
  diversity of competing frameworks.
  The Question Generation Theorem identifies this
  as a form of scientific pathological continuation:
  the theory survives while the science contracts.
\end{remark}


The Anomaly Preservation Theorem


\begin{theorem}{Anomaly Preservation Theorem}{anomaly-preserve}
  A healthy scientific community preserves anomalies:
  it maintains $\mathcal{F}_S \ne \emptyset$
  and ensures anomalies are available for resolution
  rather than suppressed or marginalised.
  Formally, science is generatively admissible only if
  \[
    \reco(\mathcal{F}_S, t) > 0
    \quad\text{for all } t.
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Repair Existence Theorem
  (\cref{thm:repair-exist}), repair is possible iff
  recoverability is positive. Scientific repair
  (theory revision) operates on anomalies: it requires
  that the anomalous observations are recoverable from
  the scientific record.

  If $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S, t) = 0$, anomalies have
  passed the scientific semantic horizon: they are
  no longer accessible to the repair algebra.
  This occurs when anomalous results are unpublished,
  retracted without cause, or buried in inaccessible
  archives.
  In this case $\mathcal{R}_S$ cannot act on
  $\mathcal{F}_S$, and the failure manifold cannot shrink.
  The science stagnates: it cannot repair distinctions
  that have lost recoverability.

  Conversely, if $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S, t) > 0$,
  anomalies remain accessible and the repair algebra
  can act on them. This is the necessary condition for
  scientific progress.
\end{proof}

Recall the Missing Stair example from
Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction}: residents of an old house step
over a broken stair without noticing it, while visitors trip
on it immediately. Scientific communities accumulate
invisible distinctions in exactly the same way. An anomaly
that a graduate student notices on first contact with a field
is often one its senior practitioners have long since learned
to step around without seeing. Anomaly preservation, in the
formal sense above, is partly a problem of keeping such
anomalies recoverable for newcomers who have not yet learned
where the stair is.

\begin{remark}{Replication crisis as recoverability failure}
  {replication-crisis}
  The replication crisis in psychology, medicine, and
  social science is formally a recoverability failure.
  Non-publication of null results drives $\reco$
  toward zero for the null result distinctions. The
  published literature over-represents positive
  findings, creating a biased failure manifold that
  the repair algebra cannot act on because the
  anomalous (null) results are not recoverable from
  the available record.

  The Anomaly Preservation Theorem predicts that
  pre-registration, mandatory reporting of null results,
  and open data requirements all increase
  $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S)$ and therefore restore the
  condition for scientific repair.

  The framework isolates the \emph{recoverability
  dimension} of the crisis. Other contributing factors
  --- incentive structures (publish-or-perish pressures),
  statistical power failures (underpowered studies with
  inflated effect sizes), methodological monoculture
  (over-reliance on $p < 0.05$ as the sole repair
  criterion), and laboratory-to-lab variation in
  experimental protocols --- are not directly captured
  by $\reco$ alone, though they interact with it.
  A full account of the replication crisis requires
  all these dimensions; the distinction-theoretic
  account addresses the informational dimension specifically.

  Importantly, however, the framework's claim is not
  exclusivity but \emph{unification}: many of these
  mechanisms can themselves be interpreted as distinction
  failures under the vocabulary developed here. Incentive
  structures that suppress null-result publication are
  \emph{repair-algebra capture}: the gatekeeping
  mechanisms of $\mathcal{R}_S$ are controlled by actors
  whose interests misalign with the full failure manifold
  $\mathcal{F}_S$ (\cref{defn:gov-ecology}). Low
  statistical power is a \emph{distinction-capacity}
  failure: the experimental design cannot distinguish
  between a true null and a false null, collapsing two
  regions of $\mathcal{D}_S$ that should be separate.
  Researcher degrees of freedom are a form of
  \emph{administrative projection}
  (\cref{thm:admin-blind}): the researcher's hypothesis
  space is projected onto the data in a way that
  systematically hides its own blind spots. The
  distinction-theoretic account does not compete with
  these analyses; it provides a common vocabulary in
  which they can be stated and compared.
\end{remark}


The Regenerative Epistemology Theorem


\begin{theorem}{Regenerative Epistemology Theorem}{regen-epistem}
  A scientific tradition is regenerative iff:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item It preserves the recoverability of anomalies:
          $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S, t) > 0$.
    \item It trains new practitioners who can exercise
          the full repair algebra $\mathcal{R}_S$.
    \item It preserves methodological diversity:
          $N(\mathcal{R}_S) \ge N_{\min}$ independent
          repair pathways.
    \item It maintains a non-trivial failure manifold:
          $\mathcal{F}_S \ne \emptyset$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Principle of Regeneration
  (\cref{prc:regen-prc}), a system is regenerative
  iff it preserves repair capacity.

  (i) preserves recoverability, which by the Repair
  Existence Theorem is necessary for any repair.
  (ii) preserves the ability to \emph{exercise} the
  repair algebra — a repair algebra that cannot be
  operated is operationally zero capacity.
  (iii) by the Diversity--Repair Theorem
  (\cref{thm:diversity-repair}), multiple independent
  repair pathways maximise repair capacity; losing
  methodological diversity reduces $\kappa(\mathcal{S})$.
  (iv) an empty failure manifold ($\mathcal{F}_S = \emptyset$)
  means no anomalies are recognised. This cannot arise
  in a healthy science (every partition produces blind
  spots, every model has anomalies) and instead
  indicates that anomalies are being suppressed rather
  than resolved — a failure of (i).

  Together (i)--(iv) are necessary and sufficient for
  the Regeneration Theorem (\cref{thm:regeneration}).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}{Healthy vs.\ Pathological Science}
  {healthy-sci}
  A healthy scientific tradition satisfies all four
  conditions of the Regenerative Epistemology Theorem.
  Physics in the late 19th century: anomalies (Mercury,
  Michelson-Morley, blackbody radiation) were
  publicly recognised and preserved; new practitioners
  were trained in classical mechanics and in the new
  experimental methods; methodological diversity was
  high (mathematical physics, experimental physics,
  theoretical chemistry); and the failure manifold
  was acknowledged as non-empty.

  A pathological scientific tradition violates at
  least one condition. Lysenkoism in Soviet biology
  violated (i) by suppressing anomalies; (ii) by
  excluding geneticists from training programmes;
  (iii) by mandating a single methodological framework;
  and (iv) by declaring the failure manifold empty
  through ideological fiat.
  The Regenerative Epistemology Theorem predicts
  total collapse of repair capacity — which occurred.
\end{example}


Science and the Full Admissibility Invariant


The four components of the full admissibility invariant
$\mathbf{I}_\adm = (\Vol, S_\adm, \chi, \kappa_\adm)$
(\cref{defn:full-adm-inv}) have direct scientific
interpretations.

\begin{proposition}{Scientific Admissibility Invariant}
  {sci-adm-inv}
  \index{admissibility!social}
  For a scientific distinction ecology $\mathcal{S}$:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item $\Vol(\adm_S)$ measures the breadth of
          approachable scientific territory.
    \item $S_\adm(\mathcal{S})$ measures the diversity
          of scientific futures: how evenly the
          approachable territory is distributed across
          distinct research directions.
    \item $\chi(\adm_S)$ measures the topological
          complexity of the admissible scientific future:
          how many disconnected research programmes
          are simultaneously viable.
    \item $\kappa_\adm(\mathcal{S})$ identifies
          high-curvature decision points in the
          scientific landscape — paradigm shifts,
          methodological revolutions, and theoretical
          unifications where small choices have
          large long-term consequences.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Each component of $\mathbf{I}_\adm$ is applied to
  the scientific policy space $P_S$ (the space of
  possible research programmes, theoretical frameworks,
  and methodologies).
  (i) is direct: admissible volume equals accessible
  research territory.
  (ii)--(iv) follow from the definitions of
  admissibility entropy, Euler characteristic, and
  curvature (\cref{ch:geometry-futures}) applied to $P_S$.
\end{proof}


Related Frameworks

\label{sec:ch29-related-frameworks}

\subsection*{Luhmann: Science as a Coded Subsystem}

\emph{Convergence.} Chapters~\ref{ch:distinction-ecology}
and~\ref{ch:governance} engaged Luhmann on operational closure in
general and on law and politics specifically; this chapter's
concern is his treatment of science. Luhmann's theory of science
treats it as a functionally differentiated, operationally closed
subsystem of society, reproducing itself through scientific
communications that reference primarily other scientific
communications, organised around its own binary code, true/false
\parencite{luhmann1990}. This matches the Scientific Distinction
Ecology defined above (\cref{defn:sci-ecology}), which likewise
treats science as a self-sustaining structure with its own
internal repair dynamics (\cref{thm:sci-repair}), not a passive
accumulation of facts about the world.

\emph{Divergence.} For Luhmann, the true/false code is largely
taken as given: science's distinctive achievement is having
settled on a binary code at all, and the theory says comparatively
little about the internal structure of the space of true and
false claims. This chapter's apparatus goes considerably further:
the Question Generation and Anomaly Preservation Theorems
(\cref{thm:question-gen,thm:anomaly-preserve}) characterise the
internal geometry of scientific distinction-production --- which
anomalies must be preserved rather than prematurely resolved, and
why --- a level of structural detail Luhmann's code-level
description does not attempt.

\emph{Open question.} Whether the requirement that a healthy
scientific ecology actively preserve unresolved anomalies can be
derived from Luhmann's claim that the true/false code is what
allows science to process communications at all, or is an
additional requirement his framework does not predict, is left
open.

\subsection*{Gunderson and Holling: The Adaptive Cycle}

\emph{Convergence.} Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction-ecology} engaged
Holling on the basin-and-threshold structure of ecological
resilience; this chapter's concern is closer to the later
panarchy model, which describes systems cycling through four
phases --- exploitation, conservation, release, and
reorganisation --- with collapse understood not as pure failure
but as a necessary phase that frees accumulated structure for
reorganisation into new configurations \parencite{gunderson2002}.
This maps closely onto this chapter's account of scientific
revolutions: a paradigm's accumulation of anomalies, followed by
crisis and restructuring, is exactly the adaptive-cycle pattern,
here applied to a scientific distinction ecology rather than an
ecosystem.

\emph{Divergence.} The adaptive cycle is a largely qualitative,
stage-based description: its phases are characterised by loose
properties --- connectedness, resilience, potential --- without a
single quantity tracked continuously across the whole cycle. The
Regenerative Epistemology Theorem (\cref{thm:regen-epistem})
instead ties scientific health to one continuously tracked
quantity, admissible volume $\Vol(\adm_S)$, with the Anomaly
Preservation Theorem showing that what panarchy calls premature
conservation-phase rigidity is formally a special case of
admissibility contraction --- a unified account where panarchy
posits four qualitatively distinct phases.

\emph{Open question.} Whether panarchy's four-phase structure can
be recovered as a coarse-grained reading of this chapter's
continuous $\Vol(\adm_S)$ trajectory --- phases as sign-regions of
its derivative and curvature, for instance --- or whether panarchy
captures genuine discontinuities a continuous admissibility
trajectory cannot represent, is not settled here.


\section{Extended Example: The Stair Nobody Saw ---
\emph{Helicobacter pylori} and the Ulcer Revolution}
\label{sec:ch29-hpylori}

The Missing Stair passage following the Anomaly Preservation
Theorem (\cref{thm:anomaly-preserve}) describes a pattern in the
abstract: an anomaly that senior practitioners have long since
learned to step around without seeing, recoverable in principle
but not in practice. The discovery that most peptic ulcers are
caused by a bacterium, not by stress or excess acid, is that
pattern with names, dates, and a documented cost paid by one
specific person to force the anomaly back into recoverability.


The Distinction Ecology of Peptic Ulcer Disease Before 1982


Cast as a scientific distinction ecology $\mathcal{S} =
(\mathcal{D}_S, \mathcal{L}_S, \mathcal{R}_S, \mathcal{F}_S,
\mathcal{Q}_S)$ (\cref{defn:sci-ecology}), gastroenterology's
working theory of peptic ulcer disease through the 1970s held
$\mathcal{D}_S$ fixed around stress, diet, and gastric acid as
the causal triad. $\mathcal{R}_S$, the repair algebra, was real
and active, but it operated entirely within that triad:
antacids, bed rest, and bland diets gave way in 1976 to
cimetidine (Tagamet), the first drug to block acid production at
its histamine-receptor source, and severe or recurrent cases
went to surgery --- vagotomy or partial gastrectomy, removing or
disconnecting the nerve or tissue responsible for acid output.
Cimetidine became the world's first ``blockbuster'' drug,
passing one billion dollars in annual sales by 1986. None of
this repair activity touched $\mathcal{Q}_S$, the question space,
in the sense the Question Generation Theorem
(\cref{thm:question-gen}) requires for generative admissibility.
The field was not asking why ulcers formed or why they recurred
once acid suppression stopped; it was asking how to suppress acid
more effectively, indefinitely, in a population that mostly
needed to keep taking the drug or risk relapse. A repair algebra
that profitably manages a recurring symptom forever has every
incentive structure pointed away from a cure, not because anyone
intended it that way, but because nothing in $\mathcal{R}_S$
rewarded the question that would have made $\mathcal{R}_S$ itself
unnecessary.


An Anomaly Nobody Was Looking For


This is exactly the situation the Missing Stair passage
describes. Pathologists examining gastric biopsies were not
looking for bacteria, because the prevailing assumption --- correct
about the stomach's acidity in every respect except this one ---
held that no organism could survive there. Robin Warren, a
pathologist at Royal Perth Hospital, began noticing small curved
organisms in gastric biopsy slides as early as 1979
\parencite{kyle2016}. The
observation was available to anyone using a microscope on the
same tissue; what was missing was not access but a reason to look,
since standard histopathological practice did not include
checking for bacteria in a setting everyone already knew was
sterile. $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S, t)$, recoverability of this
anomaly, was technically positive --- the slides existed, the
organisms were visible --- but operationally close to zero, since
nothing in the field's training or expectations directed anyone
to recover it.


Low Recoverability, 1981--1983


In 1981, Barry Marshall, then a trainee physician on a
gastroenterology rotation, began working with Warren on the
bacteria. Early attempts to culture the organism from biopsy
material failed for the first thirty-four cases, apparently
because the cultures were discarded on the schedule used for more
familiar organisms, before this slower-growing one had time to
appear; a culture left over an Easter long weekend in 1982, by
accident of timing rather than design, finally grew it. By May
1982 Marshall and Warren had compiled data on one hundred
patients showing a strong association between the bacteria and
gastritis and ulceration. The response from the field was not
hostility so much as indifference bordering on dismissal: Marshall
presented the results to the Royal Australasian College of
Physicians in October 1982 to a mixed reception, with
gastroenterologists reportedly describing the idea that a germ
caused ulcers as simply too strange to take seriously; a
subsequent abstract submitted to the Gastroenterology Society of
Australia later that year was rejected outright
\parencite{kyle2016}. This is the
Anomaly Preservation Theorem's failure condition in its mildest,
most common form: not suppression by decree, but a repair
algebra $\mathcal{R}_S$ that the field's own gatekeeping channels
declined to engage, leaving the anomaly technically published but
practically unrecoverable.


Forcing Recoverability: The 1984 Self-Experiment


Unable to establish an animal model for the infection, Marshall
took an unusual step: in 1984, after a baseline endoscopy
confirmed a healthy stomach, he drank a culture of the organism
himself. Within three days he had developed nausea and a marked
drop in stomach acidity; a follow-up endoscopy and biopsy eight
days in confirmed active gastritis with the organism present
\parencite{marshall1985,marshall1984}.
This is worth pausing on in the chapter's terms. The normal
channels for raising $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S)$ --- publication, peer
review, professional society engagement --- had already been
tried and had not worked. What Marshall did was manufacture an
unambiguous, undeniable instance of the anomaly in his own body,
at real personal medical risk, because no other available
mechanism in $\mathcal{R}_S$ was succeeding at making the existing
evidence recoverable. The Anomaly Preservation Theorem states that
generative admissibility requires $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S, t) > 0$;
it does not promise that achieving this is free, and this case is
a record of exactly what it cost when the field's repair channels
were not doing the work on their own.


The Regenerative Epistemology Theorem, Before and After


The Regenerative Epistemology Theorem's four conditions
(\cref{thm:regen-epistem}) give a precise before-and-after reading
of this episode, in the same spirit as the chapter's existing
contrast between late-nineteenth-century physics and Lysenkoism
(\cref{ex:healthy-sci}) --- except here the contrast is the same
field, before and after 1994 rather than two different
traditions. Before: recoverability of the bacterial anomaly was
operationally near zero (condition (i) failing); practitioners
were not trained to look for or interpret it (condition (ii)
failing); the dominant repair pathway was a single one, acid
suppression, with little methodological diversity behind it
(condition (iii) marginal); and the field's working assumption
amounted to treating the failure manifold as empty --- ulcers
recurred, but recurrence was absorbed into the existing
acid-suppression framework rather than treated as evidence
against it (condition (iv) failing). After the evidence became,
in the assessment of the field's own historians, impossible to
ignore by the early 1990s, and the U.S. National Institutes of
Health convened a consensus conference in 1994 that endorsed
antibiotic eradication of \emph{H.\ pylori} as the standard
treatment for ulcer disease, all four conditions were
substantially restored: the anomaly was recoverable, new
practitioners were trained to test for the organism routinely,
multiple independent lines of evidence (microbiology, histology,
epidemiology, the self-experiment, and later controlled trials)
converged, and the failure manifold --- now including the
question of why eradication sometimes failed --- stayed visibly
non-empty rather than being defined away.


1994--2005: Ecology Restructuring


What follows 1994 is not merely a corrected fact but a
restructured $\mathcal{D}_S$. Elective ulcer surgery --- vagotomy,
partial or subtotal gastrectomy --- had been a standard, planned
intervention for recurrent peptic ulcer disease for decades; once
short-course antibiotic eradication became routine, the role of
surgery in ulcer disease had to be substantially redefined, with
the operation reserved for emergencies such as perforation rather
than as a planned response to chronic recurrence. The drug
market built around lifelong acid suppression did not disappear,
but its therapeutic role changed from primary treatment to
adjunct. And the question space did not merely reopen; it
expanded in the way the Question Generation Theorem reserves for
a \emph{great theory} ($\frac{d}{dt}|\mathcal{Q}_S(t)| \gg 0$,
\cref{thm:question-gen}): \emph{H.\ pylori} was subsequently
identified as a cause of gastric adenocarcinoma and of gastric
MALT lymphoma, opening questions in cancer etiology that had no
place in the old stress-and-acid framework at all, and later
research traced the organism's coevolution with human populations
back tens of thousands of years to the migrations out of Africa
--- a question space the 1970s ecology could not have posed because
it did not yet contain the distinction that made the question
askable. Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in
Physiology or Medicine in 2005 for the original discovery; the
prize citation is itself a late marker of $\reco(\mathcal{F}_S)$
having been fully restored, more than two decades after the
anomaly first appeared on Warren's slides.


A Caution About the Reconstruction


As with the company-town and newspaper examples of
Chapters~\ref{ch:fiscal} and \ref{ch:governance}, the dates, names,
and sequence of rejections and confirmations here are drawn from
the documented record, not invented for the analogy. What belongs
to this chapter alone is the explicit mapping onto $\mathcal{D}_S$,
$\reco(\mathcal{F}_S)$, and the four conditions of the
Regenerative Epistemology Theorem --- no journal referee in 1982
was computing a recoverability functional. What the historical
record supports without qualification is the shape: an anomaly
sitting in plain, literal, microscopic view for years; a repair
algebra with no internal incentive to look for it; a field's
normal channels failing to make it count; and a restructuring,
once it finally did, large enough to turn a stable, profitable,
century-old paradigm into a footnote in its own textbook.

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\begin{chapsummary}
  \item Science is a distinction ecology whose repair
        algebra is exercised through peer review,
        replication, and theoretical revision.
  \item Scientific repair is admissible iff revisions
        resolve anomalies, preserve existing distinctions,
        and do not introduce anomalies faster than they
        resolve them (\cref{thm:sci-repair}).
  \item Healthy science expands the question space:
        $\frac{d}{dt}|\mathcal{Q}_S| \ge 0$
        (\cref{thm:question-gen}).
  \item Anomaly recoverability is necessary for
        scientific repair; suppression of null results
        is a recoverability failure
        (\cref{thm:anomaly-preserve}).
  \item Science is regenerative iff it preserves
        anomaly recoverability, trains practitioners,
        maintains methodological diversity, and
        keeps a non-trivial failure manifold
        (\cref{thm:regen-epistem}).
  \item The four components of $\mathbf{I}_\adm$
        measure breadth, diversity, topological
        complexity, and decision sensitivity of the
        scientific future (\cref{prop:sci-adm-inv}).
\end{chapsummary}

The Argument So Far

\label{sec:ch30-arg}


Thirty chapters have been devoted to a single question
asked at progressively deeper levels of abstraction.

Chapter~\ref{ch:distinction} asked: what must exist before
anything can be observed? The answer was distinction — the
primitive act of partitioning a domain that simultaneously
produces objects, information, cost, and blind spots.

Chapter~\ref{ch:information} asked: what is information?
The answer was: the quantitative expression of distinctions,
not a primitive substance.

Chapter~\ref{ch:entropy} asked: what is entropy? The answer
was: the multiplicity hidden beneath a distinction structure,
not disorder.

Chapter~\ref{ch:history} asked: what are states? The answer
was: compressed projections of histories.

Chapter~\ref{ch:recoverability} asked: when is a lost
distinction gone forever? The answer was: only when
recoverability falls to zero — dispersal and destruction
are not equivalent.

Chapter~\ref{ch:memory} asked: what is memory? The answer
was: not storage, but preservation of recoverability.

Chapters~\ref{ch:repair}--\ref{ch:repair-intelligence}
asked: how do distinctions persist despite entropy? The
answer was: through repair — the third primitive,
irreducible to distinction and entropy, whose existence
depends on positive recoverability and whose algebra
is a monoid.

Chapters~\ref{ch:beyond-continuation}--\ref{ch:distinction-ecology}
asked: is repair enough? The answer was: no. Repair
mechanisms themselves degrade. Regeneration — preservation
of repair capacity — is necessary. And even regeneration
is insufficient unless the futures it generates remain
open. This led to the concept of distinction ecology:
a network of interacting distinctions whose structure
determines the possibility of future distinction-production.
