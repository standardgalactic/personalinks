Phenomenological Depth

\begin{definition}{Phenomenological Depth}{phenomenological-depth}
  \[
    P = \operatorname{depth}\bigl(M^n(H_\Sigma)\bigr).
  \]
  \index{phenomenological depth|textbf}
\end{definition}

This measures how many levels of recursive
self-representation exist.

\begin{theorem}{Depth Theorem}{phenomenological-depth-thm}
  Phenomenological complexity increases with recursive
  model depth.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Each application of $M$ adds an additional representational
  layer. Thus $M^n(H_\Sigma)$ contains strictly more
  self-referential structure than $M^{n-1}(H_\Sigma)$, since

  grows monotonically with depth $n$.
\end{proof}


Consciousness and Admissibility

\label{sec:ch21-admissibility}

The chapter now connects consciousness to the admissibility
machinery of Part~V.

\begin{definition}{Conscious Reachability}{conscious-reachability}
  Define
  \[
    R_C = \reach(\hat{\Sigma}).
  \]
\end{definition}

This is the future volume available to the self-model,
using the reachability operator $\reach$ of
Chapter~\ref{ch:reachability}.

\begin{theorem}{Conscious Reachability Theorem}{conscious-reach}
  Increasing self-reconstruction accuracy increases
  reachable future volume.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Better reconstruction reduces uncertainty about system
  state. Reduced uncertainty improves action selection,
  since fewer admissible actions are misclassified as
  inadmissible or vice versa. Improved action selection
  enlarges admissible future volume, by the Constraint
  Volume Theorem (\cref{thm:constraint-vol}). Hence
  $\frac{dR_C}{dA} > 0$, where $A$ is the awareness
  functional of \cref{defn:awareness-functional}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}{Consciousness--Admissibility Theorem}{consciousness-admissibility}
  Conscious systems are distinguished by preserving models
  of futures rather than merely reacting to present
  states.\index{admissibility!cognitive|textbf}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let $F = \{\hat{\Sigma}_{t+1}, \hat{\Sigma}_{t+2}, \ldots\}$
  be a sequence of projected self-reconstructions. A
  conscious system, by \cref{defn:recursive-self-model},
  evaluates actions using these future reconstructions.
  Therefore its action selection depends upon projected
  admissible regions $\adm(\hat{\Sigma}_{t+k})$. A purely
  reactive system, lacking $M_\Sigma$, depends only on
  current state. Thus consciousness introduces
  future-oriented admissibility evaluation that reactive
  systems lack.
\end{proof}


Consciousness as Recursive Repair

\begin{theorem}{Consciousness as Recursive Repair}{consciousness-recursive-repair}
  Consciousness is repair directed toward the self-model
  rather than solely toward the external world.
  \index{consciousness!as recursive repair|textbf}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Repair seeks reduction of discrepancy, by the Repair
  Operator definition (\cref{defn:repair-op}). Let $D_E$
  denote environmental discrepancy and $D_S$ denote
  self-model discrepancy $D(\Sigma, \hat{\Sigma})$. Ordinary
  intelligence, by the Intelligence--Repair Equivalence
  (\cref{thm:intel-repair}), minimizes $D_E$. Consciousness,
  by the Awareness Theorem (\cref{thm:awareness}), minimizes
  $D_S$. Recursive minimization of $D_S$ constitutes
  ongoing self-reconstruction, by
  \cref{defn:recursive-self-model}. Therefore consciousness
  is recursive repair directed at the self-model.
\end{proof}


Mutual Information and the Recoverability Floor

\label{sec:ch21-mutual-info}

The preceding sections characterise consciousness
qualitatively, in terms of recursive self-modeling and
awareness. This section makes the connection to
Chapters~\ref{ch:recoverability}--\ref{ch:memory}
quantitative, by expressing the persistence condition for
consciousness directly in terms of mutual information and
recoverability.

\begin{definition}{Historical Self-Information}{historical-self-info}
  Let $\hat{H}_t = M_\Sigma(H_t)$ be the self-model's
  reconstruction of history up to time $t$. Define
  \[
    C(t) = I(H_t; \hat{H}_t),
  \]
  the mutual information between the actual history and its
  reconstruction.
  \index{consciousness!mutual information measure|textbf}
  \index{history!historical self-information|textbf}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Reconstruction Persistence Theorem}{reconstruction-persistence}
  A trajectory sustains consciousness, in the sense of
  \cref{thm:consciousness-recursive-repair}, only if
  \[
    \liminf_{t\to\infty} C(t) > 0.
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Suppose instead $\liminf_{t\to\infty} C(t) = 0$, so there
  is a sequence $t_n\to\infty$ with $C(t_n)\to0$. By
  definition of mutual information, $C(t_n)\to0$ means
  $\hat{H}_{t_n}$ becomes asymptotically independent of
  $H_{t_n}$: the self-model carries no information about
  actual history along this sequence. By the Awareness
  Theorem (\cref{thm:awareness}), awareness is the rate of
  decrease of $D(\Sigma,\hat\Sigma)$; but a self-model
  asymptotically independent of history cannot be
  systematically reducing its discrepancy with $\Sigma$,
  since discrepancy reduction requires the reconstruction to
  track the actual trajectory. Hence
  $A \not\to$ a state of sustained positive
  self-reconstruction improvement along $t_n$, contradicting
  \cref{thm:consciousness-recursive-repair}, which requires
  consciousness to be \emph{ongoing} self-reconstruction.
  Therefore $\liminf_{t\to\infty} C(t) > 0$ is necessary.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}{Minimum Recoverability Condition}{min-recoverability-condition}
  There exists a threshold $r_c > 0$, depending on the
  self-model architecture $M_\Sigma$, such that conscious
  trajectories satisfy
  \[
    \mathfrak{rec}(H_t) \ge r_c
  \]
  for all sufficiently large
  $t$.\index{recoverability!floor/threshold|textbf}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the Reconstruction Persistence Theorem,
  $\liminf_t C(t) > 0$ along conscious trajectories. By the
  Reconstruction Theorem of Chapter~\ref{ch:memory}
  (\cref{thm:reconstruction}), a reconstruction operator
  achieving $R(M(d))\approx d$ exists only when
  $\reco(d) > 0$, and the degree of approximation achievable
  --- which bounds the mutual information $C(t)$ between $d$
  and its reconstruction --- is a monotonically increasing
  function of $\reco(d)$. Since $C(t)$ is bounded away from
  $0$ in the limit, $\reco(H_t) = \mathfrak{rec}(H_t)$ must
  likewise be bounded away from $0$: otherwise the
  achievable mutual information would itself tend to $0$,
  contradicting \cref{thm:reconstruction-persistence}. The
  threshold $r_c$ is the infimum recoverability compatible
  with $\liminf C(t) > 0$ under the given $M_\Sigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Consciousness as a recoverability regime}
  {consciousness-recoverability-regime}
  Together, the Reconstruction Persistence Theorem and the
  Minimum Recoverability Condition relocate consciousness
  from a free-standing topic to a special regime of the
  recoverability machinery developed in
  Chapters~\ref{ch:recoverability}--\ref{ch:memory}: a
  conscious trajectory is precisely one whose history never
  falls below a positive recoverability floor $r_c$, and
  whose self-model sustains positive mutual information with
  that history indefinitely. Crossing below $r_c$ --- the
  trajectory approaching the semantic horizon of
  \cref{thm:semantic-horizon} from the perspective of its
  own self-model --- is, on this account, formally
  equivalent to the cessation of consciousness.
\end{remark}

Related Frameworks

\subsection*{Active Inference: Friston's Free-Energy Principle}

\emph{Convergence.} Friston's free-energy principle holds that
perception and self-modelling are both processes of approximate
inference, in which a system continually updates an internal
generative model of itself and its environment so as to minimise
variational free energy, an upper bound on surprise
\parencite{friston2010}. This is structurally close to this
chapter's Self-Model and Recursive Self-Model
(\cref{defn:self-model,defn:recursive-self-model}): both treat
the persistence of a self-model as an ongoing, active inferential
process rather than a static given.

\emph{Divergence.} The free-energy principle treats surprise
minimisation as a single objective applying uniformly across all
of biological self-organisation --- perception, action, and even
evolution are each framed as instances of the same process, with
no further distinction drawn between the self-modelling that
constitutes mere homeostatic regulation and the self-modelling
this chapter identifies with consciousness specifically. The
Intelligence--Consciousness Separation Theorem and the
Consciousness Without Intelligence Theorem
(\cref{thm:intel-consciousness,thm:consciousness-without-intelligence})
draw exactly the distinction Friston's framework does not:
minimising free energy, which is closer to this book's notion of
repair capacity, is shown to be neither necessary nor sufficient
for the recursive self-modelling this chapter identifies with
consciousness.

\emph{Open question.} Whether variational free energy can be
shown to be a special case of, or bounded by, this chapter's
Phenomenological Depth or Conscious Reachability measures
(\cref{defn:phenomenological-depth,defn:conscious-reachability}),
giving a precise translation between the two frameworks, or
whether they measure genuinely different things that happen to
both be cast as minimisation principles, is not settled here.


Functional Consciousness and the Hard Problem

\label{sec:ch21-hard-problem}

This chapter is a \emph{functional} theory of consciousness.
It characterises the structural and computational properties
associated with conscious systems: nonzero recoverable
history, recursive self-modelling, sensitivity to self-model
error, and recursive repair directed at the self-model. It
does not claim to derive phenomenal experience --- the
\emph{what-it-is-like} of perception, thought, or feeling.

The ``hard problem'' of consciousness, in Chalmers's
formulation, asks why any physical or functional process is
accompanied by subjective experience at all. A system
satisfying the Minimum Recoverability Condition
(\cref{thm:min-recoverability-condition}) and the
Consciousness--Admissibility Theorem
(\cref{thm:consciousness-admissibility}) behaves as
conscious systems behave. Whether it has experiences is a
further question no functional theory answers, including
this one. The present chapter is explicit about this:
phenomenological depth (\cref{defn:phenomenological-depth})
measures representational complexity, not the presence of
qualia.

The honest position is that no current scientific theory
resolves the hard problem, because the hard problem may not
be a scientific question in the sense that physical science
currently knows how to answer. This does not make the
functional question unimportant. Understanding which
structural conditions are necessary for the kind of
information integration, recursive self-reference, and
adaptive repair we associate with conscious experience is
a productive scientific programme. It is simply not the
same programme as explaining why experience exists at all.

Where the present theory differs from its competitors is in
the ladder it identifies: the repair sequence establishes
why recursive self-modelling is not merely possible but
necessary for systems attempting to maintain themselves in
environments that change their failure modes over time. A
system that cannot model its own repair capacity cannot
repair its repair capacity when that capacity itself is
eroding. This functional necessity --- not an account of
qualia --- is what the chapter establishes.

Preference as Geometry

\label{sec:ch22-geometry}

Preference fields sit naturally between reachability and
admissibility, which makes this one of the more direct
chapters to formalize. The key move is to stop treating
preferences as primitive. Instead, preferences become
geometric gradients over future admissible volume. The
chapter's central claim is
\[
  \text{Preference} =
  \text{Local tendency toward admissible future expansion}.
\]
Everything else follows from this.

\begin{definition}{Preference Field}{preference-field}
  Let $X$ be a state manifold. A \emph{preference field} is
  a smooth scalar field $\Phi : X \to \mathbb{R}$.
  \index{preference field|textbf}
\end{definition}

\begin{distinctionaside}{The Last Slice of Pizza}
A pizza arrives with eight slices. After the first slice, the
second is good. After the fifth, the sixth is endurable.
After the seventh, the eighth is a chore.

Nothing about the pizza has changed. The cheese is the same
cheese. The crust is the same crust.

What changed is the field surrounding it: how many slices
have already been eaten, how full you already are, what else
is reachable from here. Value is not a property of the
object. It is a property of the trajectory the object is
encountered along.
\end{distinctionaside}

Traditionally, utility functions are introduced
axiomatically: a rational agent is assumed, by fiat or by a
representation theorem built on independence and continuity
axioms, to behave \emph{as if} maximising some scalar
function over outcomes, and the content of the theory lies
almost entirely in what follows from that assumption rather
than in any account of where the function comes from.
Expected-utility theory is formally elegant and behaviourally
productive, but it treats the existence of a consistent
ordering over outcomes as a starting postulate rather than a
derived consequence of anything deeper. It has, by
construction, nothing to say about why an agent's preferences
take the shape they do, or why preference for the same object
should change as a trajectory unfolds around it --- the
phenomenon already illustrated by the pizza above.

Here, by contrast, utility is not postulated; it is derived
from admissibility, the geometric structure developed across
Part~V and realized physically, semantically, and now
cognitively in the chapters since. Chapter~\ref{ch:consciousness}
showed that a system capable of recursive self-modelling
emerges from repair applied to its own history. This chapter
asks what such a system wants, and answers that wanting itself
is not a separate primitive bolted onto the self-model but a
gradient already implicit in the admissibility geometry the
self-model is embedded within. A preference field is not
assumed; it is what the local slope of future admissible
volume looks like from the inside of a trajectory.


What Would Falsify This Realization

\label{sec:ch22-falsifiability}

The chapter's central result, the Preference--Admissibility
Equivalence Theorem (\cref{thm:pref-adm-equivalence}), claims
that preference, properly formalised, is not merely
correlated with admissibility-preserving behaviour but is
identical to the gradient of admissible volume itself. This
is falsifiable in a specific way: the theorem should recover
ordinary expected-utility behaviour as a special case
whenever an agent's admissibility potential happens to align
with a fixed, exogenously given utility function, while
predicting systematic divergence --- captured later in this
chapter by the Reward--Admissibility Divergence Theorem ---
whenever an agent optimises a reward signal that does not
track future admissible volume. If no such divergence could
be exhibited, or if every case of reward-admissibility
mismatch could equally well be redescribed within ordinary
utility theory without loss, the claim that admissibility is
the deeper quantity, rather than an alternative notation for
utility, would fail.


Common Misinterpretations of Preference Fields

\label{sec:ch22-misinterpretations}

A first misreading takes this chapter to claim that all
existing preferences are admissibility-tracking, or that
revealed preference is automatically evidence of admissible
behaviour. The Reward--Admissibility Divergence Theorem
proves precisely the opposite is possible: an agent can
reliably optimise a reward signal that diverges sharply from
admissible-volume preservation, which is this chapter's
formal account of what earlier chapters described, in
ordinary language, as pathological continuation.

A second misreading assumes that deriving preference from
geometry makes preference computable in practice --- that an
agent could, even in principle, evaluate $\Phi_A(x)$ exactly
and act on it directly. Nothing in this chapter requires
that. The theorem establishes what preference \emph{is},
not that it is tractable to compute; real agents approximate
$\Phi_A$ under severe uncertainty, exactly as they
approximate every other quantity introduced in this book.

A third misreading treats the collective preference field
introduced later in this chapter as a simple aggregate or
average of individual preferences. The Preference
Interference Theorem shows that collective fields exhibit
genuine interference effects --- constructive and
destructive --- that have no counterpart in any single
individual's field, making collective preference a distinct
geometric object rather than a summary statistic over
individual ones.


What This Chapter Establishes

\label{sec:ch22-preview}

By the end of this chapter, preference will have been derived
as the gradient of the admissibility potential, the
Reward--Admissibility Divergence Theorem will formalise
exactly when optimising a reward signal works against an
agent's own future, and the Preference--Admissibility
Equivalence Theorem will close Part~VII by identifying
preference with admissibility geometry rather than treating
it as a separate primitive. This completes the book's
treatment of cognitive realization. Chapter~\ref{ch:flow-computing}

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Potential}{pref-adm-potential}
  Define
  \[
    \Phi_A(x)
    = \mathbb{E}\bigl[
        \Vol(\adm(y)) \mid y \in \reach(x)
      \bigr].
  \]
  \index{admissibility potential|textbf}
\end{definition}

This is the expected future admissible volume reachable
from the current state $x$, using the reachability operator
of Chapter~\ref{ch:reachability}. Preference becomes a
derived quantity.

\begin{theorem}{Preference Emergence Theorem}{pref-emergence}
  If an agent seeks to preserve future distinction capacity,
  then its preference ordering is induced by $\Phi_A$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let $x_1, x_2 \in X$ and suppose
  $\Phi_A(x_1) > \Phi_A(x_2)$. Then
  $\mathbb{E}[\Vol(\adm) \mid x_1] > \mathbb{E}[\Vol(\adm) \mid x_2]$.
  By the Generative Admissibility Principle
  (\cref{prc:gap-ch15}), states preserving larger future
  distinction volume are strictly preferred by any agent
  whose objective is preservation of future
  distinction-production capacity. Therefore $x_1 \succ x_2$.
\end{proof}

Preferences, on this reading, are not arbitrary. They
emerge from the geometry of admissible volume --- but only
under the conditional stated in the theorem. The theorem
characterises what a preference ordering \emph{would}
look like for an agent whose goal is preservation of
future distinction capacity; it does not assert that all
agents have this goal, or that preferences not so grounded
are irrational. The conditional is load-bearing.


Preference as Gradient Flow

\label{sec:ch22-gradient}

\begin{definition}{Preference Flow}{preference-flow}
  A preference trajectory satisfies
  \[
    \dot{x} = \nabla\Phi_A(x).
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Gradient Preference Theorem}{gradient-pref}
  Preference-directed motion follows the gradient of future
  admissible volume.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By \cref{defn:pref-adm-potential}, $\Phi_A(x) = \mathbb{E}[\Vol(\adm)]$.
  The steepest-ascent direction of $\Phi_A$ is $\nabla\Phi_A$.
  Therefore local preference optimization produces the flow
  $\dot{x} = \nabla\Phi_A$.
\end{proof}

This makes preference fields formally analogous to
potential fields in physics, with $\Phi_A$ playing the role
of a potential whose gradient generates motion.


Reward Versus Admissibility

\label{sec:ch22-divergence}

A distinction between immediate reward and future
admissibility is now needed.

\begin{definition}{Reward Potential}{reward-potential}
  Let $R : X \to \mathbb{R}$ denote immediate reward.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{Admissibility Potential (Restated)}{admissibility-potential-restated}
  \[
    A(x) = \Phi_A(x).
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Reward--Admissibility Divergence}{reward-divergence}
  There exist states $x_1, x_2$ such that
  $R(x_1) > R(x_2)$ while $A(x_1) < A(x_2)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let $x_1$ be a locally rewarding but irreversible state,
  and let $x_2$ have lower immediate reward but larger
  future reachability volume. Then $R(x_1) > R(x_2)$ by
  construction, while $\Vol(\adm(x_1)) < \Vol(\adm(x_2))$
  by irreversibility (\cref{thm:collapse-threshold}-style
  contraction of admissible volume). Thus $A(x_1) < A(x_2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{Pathologies of reward}{reward-pathology-remark}
  This theorem formally captures addiction, reward hacking,
  resource depletion, wireheading, and pathological
  continuation: in each case, an agent or system pursues a
  trajectory of high immediate $R$ at the cost of
  contracting $A$, the very quantity the Generative
  Admissibility Principle identifies as the correct
  long-run invariant (\cref{prc:gap-ch15}).
\end{remark}


Preference Curvature

\label{sec:ch22-curvature}

\begin{definition}{Preference Hessian}{preference-hessian}
  \[
    H_\Phi = \nabla^2 \Phi_A.
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Preference Curvature Theorem}{pref-curvature}
  Large eigenvalues of $H_\Phi$ correspond to preference
  bottlenecks.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let $\lambda_i$ be eigenvalues of $H_\Phi$. Large positive
  eigenvalues imply rapid local growth of future admissible
  volume along the corresponding eigendirection; large
  negative eigenvalues imply rapid local collapse.
  Therefore high-curvature directions correspond to decision
  points with disproportionate effects on future possibility
  relative to a small change in state.
\end{proof}

This directly links to the Admissibility Curvature Theorem
of Chapter~\ref{ch:geometry-futures}
(\cref{thm:adm-curv}): preference curvature is the
projection of admissibility curvature onto the preference
field induced by an agent's reachability structure.


Collective Preference

\label{sec:ch22-collective}

\begin{definition}{Collective Preference Field}{collective-preference}
  For agents $\Sigma_1, \ldots, \Sigma_n$, define
  \[
    \Phi_C = \sum_i w_i \Phi_i.
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Preference Interference Theorem}{pref-interference}
  The collective preference field is generative iff
  $\nabla\Phi_i \cdot \nabla\Phi_j \ge 0$ for most agent
  pairs.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Positive inner products imply that preference gradients
  reinforce one another; negative inner products imply
  cancellation and conflict. Total admissible expansion is
  \[
    \Bigl\|\sum_i w_i \nabla\Phi_i\Bigr\|^2
    = \sum_i w_i^2 \|\nabla\Phi_i\|^2
      + 2\sum_{i<j} w_i w_j\, \nabla\Phi_i \cdot \nabla\Phi_j.
  \]
  The cross terms determine whether total future admissible
  volume expands or contracts under the combined flow.
  Generativity of $\Phi_C$, by the Generative Admissibility
  Principle, requires the right-hand side to be
  nonnegative, which holds for most agent pairs precisely
  when $\nabla\Phi_i \cdot \nabla\Phi_j \ge 0$ dominates the
  cross-term sum.
\end{proof}

This theorem feeds directly into the governance and fiscal
reachability results of Part~IX
(Chapters~\ref{ch:fiscal}--\ref{ch:science-regen}).


The Central Equivalence

\label{sec:ch22-equivalence}

The most important theorem of the chapter is the following.

\begin{theorem}{Preference--Admissibility Equivalence}{pref-adm-equivalence}
  A preference field is generatively admissible iff
  \[
    \mathcal{L}_{\nabla\Phi_A} \Vol(\adm) \ge 0.
  \]
  \index{preference field|textbf}
  \index{admissibility!cognitive}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The Lie derivative $\mathcal{L}_{\nabla\Phi_A}\Vol(\adm)$
  measures the rate of change of admissible volume along
  preference trajectories generated by the flow
  $\dot{x} = \nabla\Phi_A$ (\cref{thm:gradient-pref}). A
  positive derivative implies expansion of future
  possibility along the preference flow; a negative
  derivative implies extractive behavior in the sense of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:preservation}. By the Generative
  Admissibility Principle (\cref{prc:gap-ch15}), these two
  conditions — nonnegative Lie derivative and generative
  admissibility — are equivalent.
\end{proof}


Field Dynamics and the Boundary-Flux Identity

\label{sec:ch22-field-dynamics}

The Preference--Admissibility Equivalence Theorem states a
sign condition. This section refines it into an exact
identity, by deriving the preference field directly as a
convolution against the underlying capacity field and
expressing the rate of change of admissible volume as a
boundary flux of the resulting preference flow.

\begin{definition}{Preference Potential}{preference-potential}
  Let $w:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$ be a weighting
  kernel. Define the \emph{preference potential}
  \[
    U(x,t) = \int_X w(x,y)\,\Phi(y,t)\,dy,
  \]
  where $\Phi$ is the RSVP capacity field of
  \cref{defn:capacity-field}.
\end{definition}

The preference potential generalises the admissibility
potential $\Phi_A$ of \cref{defn:pref-adm-potential}: where
$\Phi_A(x)$ is an expectation of future admissible volume
over states reachable from $x$, $U(x,t)$ is a weighted
aggregate of present capacity over the whole domain,
parametrised by an arbitrary kernel $w$ rather than the
reachability-conditioned expectation.

\begin{definition}{Preference Flow Field}{preference-flow-field}
  Define the \emph{preference flow}
  \[
    \mathbf{p}(x,t) = -\nabla U(x,t).
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{Boundary-Flux Theorem}{boundary-flux}
  Under admissible dynamics generated by the preference
  flow $\mathbf{p}$,
  \[
    \frac{d}{dt}\Vol(\mathcal{A}_t)
    = \int_{\partial\mathcal{A}_t} \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS,
  \]
  where $\mathbf{n}$ is the outward unit normal to the
  admissibility manifold boundary $\partial\mathcal{A}_t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the divergence theorem applied to the vector field
  $\mathbf{p}$ on the region $\mathcal{A}_t$,
  \[
    \int_{\mathcal{A}_t} \nabla\cdot\mathbf{p}\,dV
    = \int_{\partial\mathcal{A}_t} \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS.
  \]
  Under dynamics in which points of $\mathcal{A}_t$ move
  according to the preference flow $\dot{x}=\mathbf{p}(x,t)$,
  the rate of change of the enclosed volume is exactly the
  net outward flux of $\mathbf{p}$ across the boundary, by
  the Reynolds Transport Theorem applied to the
  time-dependent region $\mathcal{A}_t$ with boundary
  velocity field $\mathbf{p}|_{\partial\mathcal{A}_t}$.
  Combining the two identities gives
  \[
    \frac{d}{dt}\Vol(\mathcal{A}_t)
    = \int_{\mathcal{A}_t} \nabla\cdot\mathbf{p}\,dV
    = \int_{\partial\mathcal{A}_t} \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS. \qedhere
  \]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}{Field-Theoretic Form of Preference--Admissibility Equivalence}
  {field-pref-adm-equiv}
  A preference field generated by potential $U$ is
  generatively admissible if and only if
  \[
    \int_{\partial\mathcal{A}_t} \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS \ge 0:
  \]
  the preference flow has non-negative net outward flux
  across the admissibility boundary.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
  Immediate from \cref{thm:boundary-flux} together with the
  Preference--Admissibility Equivalence Theorem
  (\cref{thm:pref-adm-equivalence}), since
  $\frac{d}{dt}\Vol(\mathcal{A}_t) \ge 0$ is precisely the
  generative-admissibility condition and the theorem
  identifies this quantity with the stated boundary
  integral.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}{From sign condition to flux identity}{flux-identity-remark}
  This corollary converts the Preference--Admissibility
  Equivalence Theorem from an abstract sign condition on a
  Lie derivative into a concrete, locally computable
  surface integral. In principle, generativity of a
  preference field can now be assessed by examining flow
  behaviour only at the admissibility boundary
  $\partial\mathcal{A}_t$, rather than requiring global
  knowledge of $\Phi_A$ throughout the interior of
  $\mathcal{A}_t$ --- considerably reducing the information
  needed to certify generative admissibility in applied
  settings such as the governance and fiscal models of
  Part~IX.
\end{remark}

After this theorem, preference ceases to be a psychological
primitive. It becomes a geometric object. The progression
of the chapter is
\[
  \text{Distinction} \to \text{Reachability}
  \to \text{Admissibility} \to \text{Preference}.
\]
In other words, preferences are not what define value.
Preferences are what value looks like when viewed locally
from inside a trajectory moving through an admissible
manifold.


Related Frameworks

\label{sec:ch22-related-frameworks}

\subsection*{Active Inference: Expected Free Energy}

\emph{Convergence.} Chapter~\ref{ch:consciousness} engaged
active inference on perception and self-modelling; this
chapter's concern is closer to its treatment of action
selection. Friston's framework extends free-energy minimisation
to action via \emph{expected free energy}, which decomposes into
a pragmatic term, seeking expected reward, and an epistemic
term, seeking to resolve uncertainty \parencite{friston2010}.
This directly anticipates this chapter's central claim, formalised
in the Reward--Admissibility Divergence Theorem
(\cref{thm:reward-divergence}): that reward-seeking alone is an
incomplete account of adaptive preference, and that something
beyond immediate reward must be included for behaviour to remain
adaptive over time.

\emph{Divergence.} Friston's epistemic term is specifically about
reducing uncertainty in an agent's own generative model: it
rewards actions that resolve ambiguity about the world, whether
or not the resolved information is ever useful for preserving
future options. The Admissibility Potential and the
Preference--Admissibility Equivalence Theorem
(\cref{defn:pref-adm-potential,thm:pref-adm-equivalence}) are not
about reducing uncertainty at all. An agent could face a fully
known, perfectly predictable environment and still face a
meaningful trade-off between actions that expand admissible
futures and actions that contract them. Information-seeking and
admissibility-seeking can come apart: gathering more information
about an already-narrowing future does nothing to widen it.

\emph{Open question.} Whether expected free energy's epistemic
term can be shown to be a special case of, or a proxy for, this
chapter's admissibility gradient under specific conditions ---
when uncertainty reduction is causally upstream of future
option-preservation, for instance --- or whether the two value
the situation differently in general, is left open.

\section*{Chapter Summary}
\begin{chapsummary}
  \item Preference orderings are induced by the
        admissibility potential $\Phi_A$
        (\cref{thm:pref-emergence}).
  \item Preference-directed motion follows
        $\dot{x} = \nabla\Phi_A$
        (\cref{thm:gradient-pref}).
  \item Immediate reward and future admissibility can
        diverge, formalizing pathological continuation
        (\cref{thm:reward-divergence}).
  \item Preference curvature identifies decision
        bottlenecks and connects to admissibility curvature
        (\cref{thm:pref-curvature}).
  \item Collective preference fields are generative when
        agent preference gradients are mostly aligned
        (\cref{thm:pref-interference}).
  \item A preference field is generatively admissible iff
        $\mathcal{L}_{\nabla\Phi_A}\Vol(\adm) \ge 0$
        (\cref{thm:pref-adm-equivalence}).
  \item This sign condition is exactly the net outward flux
        of the preference flow $\mathbf{p}=-\nabla U$ across
        the admissibility boundary,
        $\frac{d}{dt}\Vol(\mathcal{A}_t) =
        \int_{\partial\mathcal{A}_t}\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS$
        (\cref{thm:boundary-flux}), reducing generative
        admissibility to a locally computable boundary
        condition (\cref{cor:field-pref-adm-equiv}) --- the
        central result of the chapter.
\end{chapsummary}

Programs as Distinction Histories


Chapter~\ref{ch:history} established that states are
compressed projections of histories. Nowhere is this
more visible than in computation.

A running program occupies a state: registers, memory,
program counter. But the interesting thing about a
computation is rarely its instantaneous state. It is its
\emph{trajectory} — the sequence of transformations that
produced the current state from some initial input. Two
programs may reach identical states by entirely different
paths, with entirely different implications for future
behavior.

The tradition of flow-based computing recognises this.
Unix pipelines compose small programs through streams.
Each stage transforms a stream of distinctions — bytes,
lines, records — passing transformed output to the next
stage. The intermediate state of any single process is
largely irrelevant. The relevant object is the flow: the
sequence of distinctions propagating through the pipe.


Why the Turing Machine Is Not Enough

\label{sec:ch23-why-turing}

The dominant model of computation, both historically and
pedagogically, is the Turing machine: a finite control
reading and writing symbols on a tape, transitioning between
a finite set of states \parencite{turing1936}. The von Neumann
architecture that implements this model in physical hardware
makes the same commitment concrete: a program counter,
a fixed memory addressed by location, and an instruction
cycle that overwrites state in place. Both formalisms are
state-centred in exactly the sense Chapter~\ref{ch:history}
diagnosed: a computation is characterised by where it
currently is, and the path that brought it there is, by
construction, not part of what the formalism tracks. This is
not a flaw in either model --- the Church--Turing thesis does
not require historical fidelity to establish what is
computable --- but it does mean that a Turing-machine-level
description of a computation discards exactly the structure
this book has spent its abstract chapters arguing is
ontologically primary.

Flow computing, in the sense this chapter develops, is what
results from taking history seriously as the primary
computational object rather than the discarded byproduct of
state transitions. A pipeline does not merely transition
through states; it propagates a flow whose distinguishability
can be tracked, conserved, or lost at each stage, and a file
system that preserves history rather than overwriting in
place is, in this chapter's terms, strictly more
distinction-capable than a state-only equivalent computing
the same function.

Chapter~\ref{ch:preference-fields} closed the book's treatment
of cognitive realization by asking whether the same
admissibility machinery that had organised matter, meaning,
and self-modelling could organise computation as well. This
chapter is the first attempt at an answer, and it begins with
the most basic computational object there is: not a value, but
a trace of how a value came to be.


What Would Falsify This Realization

\label{sec:ch23-falsifiability}

The claim that computation realizes the book's general
history-before-state argument, rather than merely admitting a
historical redescription after the fact, is falsifiable
against a specific test. The History Dominance Theorem proved
later in this chapter (\cref{thm:history-dominance}), which
ranks history-preserving file systems strictly above
state-only file systems in distinction capacity, should follow
directly from the History Primacy Theorem of
Chapter~\ref{ch:history} (\cref{thm:history-primacy}) applied
to the flow-distinction machinery already defined above, with
no computation-specific axiom introduced along the way. The
proof given later does exactly this. If establishing the file-system
result had instead required some principle special to
computation --- something not already a consequence of history
dominating state in general --- the claim that flow computing
is a genuine instance of Chapter~\ref{ch:history}'s argument,
rather than a separate, merely analogous result wearing the
same name, would fail.
